Game prompt: a platformer where you play as a frog
the game: The game is a real-time platformer in which the player is a small, wet frog moving through rain-soaked garden and city-edge places toward a moonlit rooftop pond. The player uses crouch-hops, sticky pads, tongue probes, committed tongue anchors, water, currents, and physical changes to the world to find routes upward. Places are handcrafted, connected, bounded places, not an open world. Each place has exactly one Answering Node: a stable perch beside a visible water body or rooftop perch that has a live Answering chorus. A place is completed only when the frog holds a croak from that Answering Node while a stable return route already exists from it, except for the final rooftop pond, which needs no return route and ends the game when called. Animals, rain, wind, sprinklers, rain barrels, currents, weak surfaces, and edible insects are read through physical tells, materials, noise, motion, cover, and timing. There is no combat, death, survival meter, collectathon, or abstract puzzle machine. The game is about noticing the world at the size of a frog: a puddle is a lake, a gutter is a canyon, a cat is a disaster, and a branch is a bridge.
view: Low-angle side-view platformer with layered parallax and a close camera held at frog scale; foreground vines, rain, water, and wet surfaces read depth, while the next place’s goal is visible as a distant silhouette, pond, rooftop, or faint chorus.
time: Real-time continuous. No turns, timers, pause-time actions, or menu-time traversal.
a run: A run begins under a dripping gutter, where the frog has just woken on a leaf floating in a rain barrel. A drop hits the leaf and the leaf rocks, teaching the player that this body is light, slippery, and easily moved. The player moves to the right; the frog hops. It does not run. The first gap is short and forgiving. The player learns crouch, release, land, stick, slide, tongue-grab, swing, climb, and avoid. The goal is always visible in a distant shape: a rooftop pond, a ring of lilies, a faint chorus. A run ends when the frog reaches the moonlit rooftop pond, holds a croak there, the full chorus answers, and the frog settles. If the frog cannot call, the game does not end. As the run continues, places become more vertical, more connected, and more dependent on combining verbs: entering a rain barrel, climbing sticky tiles, exiting through a drain, swinging from a branch, crossing lily pads while a heron patrols, waiting out a cat, using a sprinkler, pulling a cork, floating a leaf, or using a wasp effect. Optional routes may lead to higher places early, but they are made steep, exposed, and hazard-heavy. The game stops teaching and starts trusting.
tone: Quiet, wet, patient, and physical. The frog is “small, wet,” not “a human wearing frog skin,” not “a cartoon adventurer with a sword,” not “a realistic animal documentary.” The game teaches “reading, timing, and how much the frog’s own body can be trusted.” It is “forgiving but never weightless.” Failure produces “embarrassment, delay, and a forced rethink of the route,” not death or score shame.
look and sound: Readable low-scale wet materials: rain droplets, leaf rocking, wet shine, dripping vines, gurgling pipes, splashes, tongue taps, cat steps, heron shadows, distant frog chorus, and no HUD markers for anchors, goals, hazards, or completion.
core mechanics:
- Frog Body and Hop: The player controls a small, wet frog. It cannot run. Hold a control and the frog crouches, legs coiling; release and it launches in an arc. The hop is about aim and commitment, not speed. Once the frog leaves the ground it must live with the result. The body is light, slippery, and easily moved. The frog has sticky toe pads, bulging eyes that follow threats, a throat that swells when nervous, and a tongue that can grab, yank, and pull. There are no hunger, thirst, stamina, combat, or health meters.
- Tongue: The tongue has two registers. A light grab catches anything small enough to eat. A light tongue flick is also a probe. If the target is food-sized and the tongue can curl around it, the tongue catches and the frog eats it. If the target is too thick, too solid, or not food-sized, the tongue tip taps and releases; it cannot swallow it and does not commit the body. The tap gives material feedback: safe objects give a firm recoil and stay still; weak objects flutter, crack, droop, or snap; unusable objects slide, splash, or ignore the tongue. A safe anchor may show a brief sticky contact that releases under the tongue’s own weight, telling the player it can hold the frog if committed. A committed anchor needs something with structure enough to support the frog’s weight. Once attached, the tongue becomes a short, taut line. The frog hangs from it. Left or right creates a pendulum swing. Releasing at the right moment launches the body. If the anchor is close and strong, holding up can pull the frog toward it. If the anchor is a small movable object, a quick yank can tilt, drag, or disturb that object. If the object is too heavy, it does not move. If it is weak, it bends, snaps, or lets go under weight. The anchor ends when the player releases, the object fails, the tongue overextends, the frog is hit by a hazard, or the frog reaches a stable surface and grabs it with its pads. Safe anchors are solid, thick, damp, textured things with visible grain or edges: wet wood, braided hose, metal grates, stone lips, thick vines, strong spider silk. Weak anchors are dry, torn, translucent, fraying, loose, or too light: dry leaves, paper, cracked tile, rotten stems, thin webs, loose string. Unusable anchors are smooth, slick, hollow, or open: glass, polished plastic, smooth bare metal, water, air. The tongue cannot stick to smooth glass or open water. There are no UI markers for anchors. The player learns by material language and safe testing.
- Grip and Climbing: A surface is climbable if it is wet and textured enough for sticky pads: mossy stone, damp brick, rough bark, rubber, dry textured metal, metal grates, or other surfaces that read as grippy. Dry, smooth, oily, polished, or water-filmed surfaces are not climbable; they cause a slide or a fall. Greenhouse metal under condensation, running droplets, or a wet sheen is slippery and causes the pads to slide, even if dry textured metal elsewhere can grip. When the frog’s pads meet a climbable surface, it automatically enters Grip. A stable wall grip can be rested on. Moving up makes the frog slide upward. Moving down makes it slide down. Jumping releases it. Some surfaces are temporary: they allow Grip for a short time, then Slip, then Fall. Crumbling ledges, rotten bark, cracked tiles, and loose moss can break under the frog. Hazard contact can also break Grip. The player learns the difference by reading material: stable surfaces hold, temporary surfaces sag or crack, and nonclimbable surfaces reject the pads.
- Water and Wet Surfaces: Water is a state, not a separate minigame. When the frog enters water, it floats at the surface and can paddle in short, weak strokes. It can submerge briefly, but cannot jump or stick until it is out. Currents are directional pushes that add to the frog’s motion. Swimming against them is hard, and riding them can be useful. The frog can exit only by reaching an edge it can grip. Smooth or dry edges cause a slip. Damp textured edges let it haul itself up. If no edge is reachable, the current carries the frog to a guaranteed safe deposit or the nearest stable perch. Puddles can become traversable through floating leaves, debris, raised water, or carried objects, but open water itself is not a platform.
- Animal Attention: The cat has four states: Unaware, Watching, Prowling, and Strike. It notices the frog only if the frog moves inside its line of sight or makes noise: hopping, splashing, tongue snaps, falling objects, scraping on metal. Motion and noise raise Watching. Stillness, cover, and distance lower it. If Watching gets high enough, the cat enters Prowling and steps closer. If the frog stays exposed near it, the cat Strikes. The player changes this by freezing, hiding behind leaves, moving behind a barrier, or making a louder noise somewhere else to pull the cat’s attention. If a cat catches the frog, the cat bats it away gently but humiliatingly, and the frog is reset to the last safe perch. The heron has three states: Hunting, Shadowed, and Strike. It notices open ground. If the frog is in an exposed area without overhead cover, the heron’s shadow drifts over that ground and attention rises. Moving in the open raises it faster. Cover, waiting in shadow, and moving from covered place to covered place lower it. The heron Strikes when attention is high and the frog is exposed. The player can distract it by disturbing a branch, starting a sprinkler, knocking something, or making it look elsewhere. If a heron snaps at the frog, there is a brief dark swallow, then the frog is coughed up at the nearest safe perch outside the heron’s strike zone. Fish and pond hazards notice movement and ripples. Stillness, cover, and lily roots reduce attention. Open swimming raises it. A fish lunge bumps the frog toward a current or edge, not into death.
- Failure Recovery and Safe Perch: A missed tongue leaves the frog dangling for a beat before it falls. A bad hop makes the frog slide off a wet tile or land in a puddle. If a cat catches the frog, it is batted away and placed back at the last safe perch. If a heron catches it, the frog is coughed up at the nearest safe perch outside the heron’s strike zone. If the frog enters water, it can swim poorly toward the nearest stable edge. If there is no obvious edge nearby, the current carries it to a guaranteed side, or the game returns it to the last stable node. There is no unresolved fall. If a hazard would leave the frog with nowhere to go, the game nudges the hazard, moves the frog to a rescue ledge, or places it at the nearest stable object. A safe perch is the most recent stable, weight-bearing contact that is not part of a moving hazard, not in a hazard’s active attack zone, and not structurally failing: a dry ledge, sturdy branch, stone lip, cork, stable wall grip, lily root, or similar object that would plausibly hold a frog. The “last safe perch” is the nearest such point that still exists and is still safe. There is no death screen, no score shame, and no harsh punishment.
- Answer Call and Place Completion: The call is a held croak, not a menu prompt. It answers only at an Answering Goal: a visible water body or perch with a live chorus or another frog that will respond. Other ponds, puddles, and perches are silent, blocked, dangerous, or decorative; calling at them only produces a normal croak. A place decides its goal by having exactly one place that answers. The player learns this by listening, by the goal’s call being heard from the route, and by the failure of calls elsewhere. The visible water body is the landmark, not the completion node. The Answering Node is the specific stable perch that belongs to the chorus: a stone lip, lily root, mossy edge, stable cork, gutter lip, or other stable surface beside the water. The frog cannot call from open water. If it is in water, it can hear the chorus and paddle toward the Answering perch, but it must haul itself onto a stable edge before holding the croak. If the only edge near a water body is weak, slippery, moving, or inside a fish strike zone, it is not an Answering Node until the frog makes it stable or reaches a stable edge. A place is completed only when three things are true: the perch has a live Answering chorus, the frog can hold the croak there, and a stable return route exists from that perch. The order is: make or discover a stable return route from the Answering Node, then call. If the frog calls before such a route exists, the Answering Node stays silent: no answering chorus, no settling, no completion. The player knows the place is incomplete because the Answering Node’s call does not resolve and the old place’s soundscape continues. If the player creates a route after a failed call, they must call again. If the route already exists, one successful call completes the place and no second call is needed. A stable return route is a continuous, survivable path back from the Answering Node to a previous safe perch using only stable anchors, climbable surfaces, safe drops, and currents that can be exited. A safe drop is a fall from a stable perch to a stable landing where the frog is not forced into a hazard, not thrown into open water with no exit, not landed on a weak, slippery, or moving surface, and not dropped into an active cat, heron, fish, sprinkler, current, or crumbling area. An unsafe drop may still be survivable, but it is not a stable return route because it forces delay, embarrassment, or another hazard. A safe surface is a stable anchor or climbable surface that is not currently failing, not covered by hazard, and not too slippery for the frog’s pads. A safe current is one the frog can enter, ride, and exit without being carried into a hazard or swept past all stable edges. An unsafe current has no exit, is too strong, or carries the frog into a hazard. The frog can change unsafe things into safe ones by physical cause: float a leaf to make an exit in a current, pull a cork to block a drain, wet a textured wall so it can be gripped, drag a branch into a gap, knock a hazard out of the route, or distract an animal so the area becomes passable. The safe way back is a precondition, not a completion reward. Physical changes that make a return route safe may persist, so completed places remain revisitable. If no physical route exists, the place is not considered completed until the frog has created or found a safe way back. The held croak is a loud noise action. It raises the attention of cats, herons, and other hazards within hearing range, and it can be used to distract them: calling from behind cover can pull a cat’s Watching toward the frog’s perch or make a heron turn toward the sound. It is not safe by default. Answering Goals are either free of active hazards, or the hazards are arranged so the player must wait, hide, or distract them before calling. If a hazard interrupts the held croak before the Answering Node responds, the place remains incomplete. The frog recovers by the normal hazard rules. The interrupted call raises hazard attention, but that attention decays if the frog is still, hidden, or away. To try again, the frog must return to the Answering Node after making the immediate area safe by waiting, distracting, or physically changing the hazard. Once a place is completed, its Answering Node becomes a Rest Node. The frog can call there again, but it only gets a friendly chorus response, not another place completion. It no longer functions as an Answering Goal for that place. It is a safe perch: stable, free of active hazard routines in its immediate area, and usable as a starting point for revisits. If the frog moves away from the Rest Node and calls, normal hazard noise rules apply again. A route into a new area is a Threshold: a narrow physical passage such as a drain, window, vine, gutter, or gap that crosses into a different bounded place with its own Answer Node, start perch, ambient call, and hazard set. Crossing it begins the next place because the old place’s soundscape and hazards end and the new place’s begin. Any path that stays within the same Answer Node’s soundscape is just another traversal route, not a transition. The moonlit rooftop pond is the final Answering Node because it is the only place with a full chorus and no onward Threshold. It is exempt from the return-route rule because it is not a place to be left. If the frog reaches the moonlit pond, holds the croak, and the full chorus answers, the game ends immediately, even if there is no way back. If the frog cannot call, the game does not end.
supporting mechanics:
- Eating Body States: Eating is never required, never scored, and never permanent. It is a body state, not progression. Eating changes the body for a moment, but the game never asks the frog to eat in order to move forward. Eating effects are not random. They happen when the frog swallows a live insect of that kind. A live fly twitching causes a sneeze: a small involuntary puff or backward recoil that can help or hinder movement. A live beetle kicking causes sluggishness: reduced speed, weaker hops, and worse control for a short time. A live wasp buzzing causes the Buzzed state: the next jump gets a sideways kick and less control. That can help the frog cross a gap if the player times it deliberately, but it can also ruin a jump if the player is careless. A dead, stunned, or knocked-down insect gives no body state. The player predicts this from the insect’s animation and controls it by choosing whether to eat, when to eat, and whether to eat live creatures. Eating can create clever traversal, but it does not unlock new abilities, fill a meter, or count toward completion.
- Reactive World: The world has three kinds of things. Static things are fixed: walls, floors, solid stone, large pipes, and permanent terrain. Reactive things can be changed by physical cause: loose corks, hinged shutters, hanging branches, floating leaves, wet surfaces, light objects, and anything with a joint or loose connection. Active things have their own behavior: animals, rain, wind, currents, sprinkler timers, and hazard routines. The frog changes things by applying plausible forces: pulling, landing, splashing, dripping, weighing on an object, eating something that affects its body, or making noise. Those changes spread only through physical contact: water wets a surface, force moves a branch, a broken object falls, vibration draws a cat, sight draws a heron, and a current moves debris. If the frog cannot touch, push, wet, break, disturb, or ride it, it is scenery. The world is not made of locked doors or abstract switches. It is made of small physical consequences. The player can use hazards: start a sprinkler to knock a caterpillar off a branch so the branch dips lower; pull a cork from a pipe so water begins to flow and carries a leaf into place; eat a wasp and get dizzy, causing a weird but useful sideways hop.
- Active Environmental Systems: No hazard begins with no physical tell. The page’s “without warning” means only that there is no interface warning, no HUD alert, and no safe countdown. The hazard still has a physical tell, but the player may miss it. A sudden hazard has a brief, local tell that is easy to ignore; a telegraphed hazard has a broad, sustained tell that gives the player time to react. If something has no physical tell at all, it is not a hazard in this game; it is scenery or a scripted moment. Sprinkler: dormant, pressurized, spraying, spent. It starts from a pressure timer, a leaking valve, or the frog pulling a cord, breaking a cork, or striking a loose part. It sprays fixed arcs, wets surfaces, knocks light objects, makes noise, and stops when pressure drains or the valve closes. Wind: calm, building, gusting, lull. It starts from open rooftop conditions or weather. It adds horizontal force to hops and swings, moves flags, leaves, and rain, ripples water, and stops after a gust. Its tells are flags, leaves, rain drift, and ripples changing before the gust. Rain barrel: stable, unbalanced, tipping, fallen. It starts from the frog’s weight on the rim, sloshing water, a tongue yank, or impact. It pours water, creates currents, floats leaves, wets surfaces, may carry the frog, and stops when it settles. Pipes and drains: gurgling, spitting, blocked, flowing, or empty. They can be changed by pulling corks, striking loose parts, blocking openings, redirecting water, or letting rain raise pressure. They can create currents, sounds, ripples, and carried debris, but their behavior is governed by physical water and pressure.
extra mechanics:
- Rain and Weather: Rain is a place-wide weather system, not a constant background effect. Its states are dry, first drops, drizzle, steady rain, downpour, and tapering. It begins when a weather front passes over the place. The tells are cloud shadow, first large drops, wind shift, and surface shine. It continues while the front covers the place and stops when the front moves away, though wetness remains afterward. Physically, rain wets surfaces: textured surfaces become climbable, smooth surfaces become slippery, metal under a water film causes slides, glass remains unusable, and puddles expand or become traversable platforms. It creates ripples, currents, floating leaves, and carried debris. It can fill gutters, start drains, float new rafts, or raise water enough to make a route possible or possible only if the player times it. It affects animals by making some seek cover and some become more alert to movement under cover. Its broad sound masks small player noises, but deliberate sounds like croaks, splashes, and metal scrapes still raise hazard attention. Rain can create routes and remove them. The player can use it by waiting, sheltering, opening a drain, redirecting water, or letting a surface become wet enough to climb.
- Soft Ladder and Revisitation: The places are not hard-locked by story gates. A later place is available if the frog can physically reach it and its Answering Node has a live chorus. The “ladder” is the readable, taught route upward: each Answering Node is heard from the next, and the next is normally reached by following that call. Optional routes to higher places are made steep, exposed, and hazard-heavy, so skipping ahead is possible but not easy. A later place reached through an optional route can be completed if it already satisfies the Answer Call and stable return route rule. If it does not, it is a hazardous dead end, not an Answering Node. What changes a hazardous dead end into a completable place is physical: a leaf raft is dragged to an edge, a cork is pulled to block a current, a branch is bent into a reachable anchor, a wet wall becomes climbable, or a falling object creates a stable landing. If there is no live chorus, it is never an Answering Node; it is only a dangerous perch. The game is not one-way levels. It is a connected set of handcrafted places, not an open world. After a place is completed, it remains reachable through stable return routes, so the player can revisit, take shortcuts, or test ideas again. What the player is expected to remember is not hidden keys, but how the place works: anchor materials, hazard routines, currents, wet surfaces, and what can be changed by touch, weight, noise, or water. The boundaries are visible and narrow, so it feels learned and bounded, not free-roaming.
content:
- rain-soaked garden and city edge seen from the ground: the game’s world; gutters, rain barrels, drainpipes, stone paths, greenhouse glass, dripping vines, and puddles are read at frog scale.
- gutters: canyon-scale routes; they carry water, can become currents, can hold wet surfaces, and can connect places as narrow physical passages.
- rain barrels: start perch, stable or unstable containers, climbable if textured, enterable through broken windows, exited through drains, and can tip when weighted, sloshed, pulled, or struck.
- drainpipes: gurgling and spitting pipes; they can carry water, create currents, make noise, block or open flows, and serve as narrow passages or hazard sources.
- stone paths: ground routes; wet stone may be slippery or climbable depending on texture and water film.
- greenhouse glass: cannot be stuck to, blocks sight or movement, reflects light, and cannot be used as an anchor or grip surface.
- dripping vines: hanging vines and wet vegetation; can be safe anchors if thick and damp, temporary if rotten, and can make surfaces wet.
- puddles that turn into platforms: water hazards that become traversable through floating leaves, debris, raised water, or carried objects; open water itself is not a platform.
- wet crates: stackable platforms and routes; solid wood crates are stable, rotten or wet-cracked crates are temporary, and they can be pushed, knocked, or climbed if textured.
- branches: safe anchors if thick and damp, swing points, yankable objects, and reactive things that can dip, break, or move under force.
- branch over a wider gap: early teaching anchor; holding the tongue control lets the frog extend, catch, and swing; wrong timing drops the frog into a puddle.
- damp wall: teaching surface; sticky pads catch, allowing slide upward against it.
- rain barrel leads to drainpipe, drainpipe leads to stack of wet crates: opening route that teaches traversal through connected garden objects.
- cat watching from distance: early teaching hazard; it is not attacking yet, but its eyes follow the frog, teaching that stillness matters.
- rooftop pond, ring of lilies, faint chorus: final goal and final Answering Node; the full chorus answers only here, and calling here ends the game.
- lily pads: floating platforms on water; they can move, tilt, sink if weak, and become routes while fish and herons make the pond dangerous.
- tin cans: resonant light objects; they make noise when touched, can distract animals, can be pushed, and may become weak anchors or floating debris.
- garden hoses: braided hose anchors, pullable objects, water sources, and routes; strong hose is safe, loose hose may move under weight.

- spider webs: strong spider silk can be a safe anchor, thin webs are weak and break, and webs can catch small insects or disturb into movement.
- koi ponds: water hazards with fish attention; movement and ripples raise fish attention, and fish lunges bump the frog toward currents or edges.
- cat doors: narrow passages and cat sight lines; they can connect areas, carry noise, and be used as cover if they break the cat’s line of sight.
- sprinklers: active hazards that can wet surfaces, make noise, knock light objects, and turn ledges into slides.
- rooftop ponds: final goal, safe water landmark, and final Answering Node.
- twigs: small anchors or light objects; safe if thick and damp, weak if dry or snapped.

- bottle caps: small grabbable objects, weak anchors, floating debris, or noise-making objects when struck.
- spider silk: strong strands can be safe anchors, thin strands are weak and snap.
- legs of insects: food-sized tongue catches; catching live insects can trigger eating body states.
- edges of cracked tiles: grabbable but weak anchors; they may break, crack, or drop the frog under weight.
- glass: smooth, unusable anchor and nonclimbable surface; it rejects tongue and pads.

- flies: live food that can cause a sneeze; they are not coins and are not required.
