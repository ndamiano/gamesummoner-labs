# The pitcher's page

Ask: `a platformer where you play as a frog`

Page to myself:

It is a platformer. You are a frog. Not a human wearing frog skin, not a cartoon adventurer with a sword, not a realistic animal documentary. The player is a small, wet frog trying to reach a moonlit rooftop pond where the frogs are calling. The world is a rain-soaked garden and city edge seen from the ground: gutters, rain barrels, drainpipes, stone paths, greenhouse glass, dripping vines, and puddles that turn into platforms.

The first minutes are quiet and wet. The game begins under a dripping gutter where the frog has just woken on a leaf floating in a rain barrel. A drop hits the leaf and the leaf rocks, teaching the player that this body is light, slippery, and easily moved. The player moves to the right; the frog hops. It does not run. Hold a control and the frog crouches, its legs coiling; release and it launches in an arc. The first gap is short and forgiving. The player learns that the jump is about aim and commitment, not speed.

A fly passes nearby. The player discovers the tongue. A quick flick snaps out, catches the fly, and the frog swallows it. The action is small and satisfying, but it also tells the player that the tongue has reach. A little farther along, there is a damp wall too high to jump onto. When the frog lands on it, its sticky pads catch. The player can slide upward against it. The wall is not a platform in the usual sense; it is a surface the frog can cling to. Then a branch hangs over a wider gap. The player holds the tongue control and the frog’s tongue extends, catching the branch. Releasing lets the frog swing. If the timing is wrong, the frog drops into a puddle. The player learns that the tongue is not just for eating. It is for moving.

The opening places are close together and legible. A rain barrel leads to a drainpipe. The drainpipe leads to a stack of wet crates. A cat watches from a distance, not attacking yet, but its eyes follow the frog. The player learns that stillness matters. Moving too much makes the cat step closer. The game is not teaching combat. It is teaching reading, timing, and how much the frog’s own body can be trusted.

The player is doing very simple things: crouch, release, land, stick, slide, tongue-grab, swing, climb, avoid. The game is asking them to turn those simple things into a path upward. The goal is always visible in a distant shape: a rooftop pond, a ring of lilies, a faint chorus. The frog is not fighting its way there. It is finding a way there.

What is working against them is the world and the frog’s own shape. The frog cannot run, so it cannot correct a bad hop by accelerating away. Its jumps are arcs, and once it leaves the ground it must live with the result. Surfaces are wet, slick, sticky, brittle, or moving. Water carries the player in currents but also slows them down. Rain barrels tip. Leaves sink. Drainpipes gurgle and spit. A heron’s shadow passes over open ground, and the player learns that open ground is dangerous. A cat can swat at the frog if it is noticed. A sprinkler can start without warning, turning a ledge into a slide. The game is made of small hazards that demand patience and observation.

The tongue is the main verb, but it is not a magic rope. It catches things that are small, grabbable, and visible: twigs, hanging wires, bottle caps, spider silk, branches, the legs of insects, the edges of cracked tiles. It cannot stick to smooth glass or open water. The player must look for anchors. A gap is not just a gap; it is a question: what can the frog catch? If the player guesses wrong, the tongue snaps out and grabs nothing, and the frog drops. If they guess slightly wrong, the tongue catches a weak object, and the object breaks or moves unexpectedly. The game is about reading a scene as a frog would: not as a map, but as a set of things you might hang from, pull, climb, or use.

When it goes wrong, the game is forgiving but never weightless. A missed tongue leaves the frog dangling for a beat before it falls. A bad hop makes the frog slide off a wet tile or land in a puddle. If a cat catches the frog, the cat bats it away gently but humiliatingly, and the frog is reset to the last safe perch. If a heron snaps at the frog, there is a brief dark swallow, then the frog is coughed up nearby. If the frog gets into water, it can swim, but poorly, and it must haul itself onto the nearest edge. There is no death screen, no score shame, no harsh punishment. The worst outcome is embarrassment, delay, and a forced rethink of the route.

After the opening phase, the game stops teaching and starts trusting. The levels become more vertical and more connected. The player is no longer asked only to cross a gap. They are asked to combine things. A rain barrel can be entered through a broken window, climbed by sticky tiles, exited through a drain, then used to swing from a branch outside. A garden path can be crossed by hopping across lily pads, but a heron patrols that pond, so the player may need to wait for a shadow to pass or disturb a nearby branch so the heron looks elsewhere. A greenhouse has glass that cannot be stuck to, but condensation makes the metal frames slippery. A rooftop has wind that changes the arc of a hop, so the player must use the tongue to anchor before jumping.

The later game is still about traversal, but the routes become richer. A level may have a direct but dangerous path and a longer but safer path. The direct path may require a chain of tongue swings over a pond. The longer path may require climbing a wall, waiting out a cat, and crossing a rain gutter. The player can also use hazards: start a sprinkler to knock a caterpillar off a branch so the branch dips lower; pull a cork from a pipe so water begins to flow and carries a leaf into place; eat a wasp and get dizzy, causing a weird but useful sideways hop. The world is not made of locked doors or switches. It is made of small physical consequences.

The particular things in this game are the frog’s body and the wet places around it. The frog has sticky toe pads, bulging eyes that follow threats, a throat that swells when it is nervous, and a tongue that can grab, yank, and pull. It can climb damp walls, slide down wet ones, swim awkwardly, and hang from things. It can eat flies, wasps, and small beetles, but eating is not a score system. Eating a wasp might make the frog hiccup and puff, which can cause a strange hop. Eating a fly might make it sneeze. These are flavor and feedback, not progress.

The world is full of frog-scale landmarks: rain barrels, lily pads, drainpipes, tin cans, garden hoses, greenhouse glass, mossy stones, wooden fence rails, hanging vines, spider webs, koi ponds, cat doors, sprinklers, and rooftop ponds. The hazards are simple and readable: a cat that watches, a heron that strikes, a fish that snaps, a sprinkler that sprays, a slippery surface, a crumbling ledge, a current that carries the frog away. The game is about noticing the world at the size of a frog. A puddle is a lake. A gutter is a canyon. A cat is a disaster. A branch is a bridge.

This game is not a combat platformer. You do not defeat enemies. You avoid them, distract them, or slip past them while they are distracted. This game is not a survival game with meters for thirst, hunger, or stamina. The frog is already a frog. The game does not pretend to simulate every animal need. This game is not a collectathon. Flies are not coins. They are part of the ecology and sometimes part of the joke, but the player is not asked to fill a counter. This game is not an open world. It is made of clear, crafted places that can be learned and traversed. This game is not a puzzle game about abstract machines. The puzzles are physical and environmental, not riddles. This game is not a human platformer with a frog costume. The movement, hazards, and routes are built from what a frog can and cannot do.
