Game prompt: a tower defense game where cats defend a fish market
the game: The game is a real-time market defense game in which the player is the head cat and keeper of a wet dockside fish market at night. The player assigns cats to posts, patrols, and alarms, places market defenses, manages fresh fish, ice, scraps, and coins, and uses the market’s own objects to keep stalls open while gulls, seagulls, raccoons, rival cats, and dock hazards raid the aisles. Cats are not obedient machines: they get distracted, hungry, frightened, curious, tired, and pulled by habits, fish, naps, other cats, and bells. The market itself is open, fragile, smelly, and full of things that fail: ice melts, fish spoils, aisles become slippery or noisy, customers complain and panic, stalls are blocked, ruined, occupied, or taken over. The player keeps the market alive by deciding what to protect, what to repair, what to let fail, and which failure to absorb. The night ends when the dawn bell rings; the player wins if the market is still open, and loses if enough stalls are lost or the market cannot serve any customer for a full raid.
view: Fixed angled overhead view of the whole market, with stalls, aisles, stock, customers, cats, enemies, defenses, and market states visible, plus clear panels for coins, fresh fish, ice, scraps, cat orders, and stall condition.
time: Real-time. Upkeep, spoilage, cat behavior, and customer patience run continuously. During a raid, enemies enter, cats fight or chase, objects break, and the player may issue emergency orders, ring the bell, or use placed defenses. Between raids, enemies are absent or leaving, and the player may build, repair, sell fish, spend coins, reassign cats, and choose what to sacrifice before the next wave.
a run: A run starts with the market waking up. The player sees the stalls, crates, aisles, sleeping cats, and fish glistening under the lamps. The market begins with stock: crates of fresh fish behind the stalls and some already laid out under the lamps. The player places initial defenses, assigns starting cats, wakes or leaves asleep starting cats, and chooses the first stall to trade. The first raid begins when the market opens and customers arrive. A run ends when the dawn bell rings. If the market is still open, the player wins. The market is open if a majority of its trade stalls are still functional and at least one customer is buying. The market is lost if a majority of stalls are ruined, closed, or occupied, or if the market cannot serve any customer for a full raid. As the night progresses, upkeep costs rise, ice melts faster, enemies bring new tactics, stalls become more fragile, customers become more nervous, and random market problems appear more often.
tone: Wet, smelly, funny fish market defended by cats. The player is the market’s keeper, not one small cat running around with health or needs. The market is alive and messy. It is not a generic medieval tower defense with wizards and castles. It is not about winning a battle on a clean path. The goal is to keep a working place alive through the night.
look and sound: Wet dockside fish market at night, lamplight, ice, fish scales, hungry animals, squawks, scrabbling, bells, wet thuds, market noise, and panicked customers.
core mechanics:
- market survival and night state: The player must keep the market trading through the night. A stall is open if it is not blocked, not occupied, and has stock available. A stall is functional if its stock is fresh enough to sell, its counter is intact, and the aisle is not so spoiled, noisy, or slippery that customers refuse to trade. A stall is closed if the player shuts it or it has no sellable stock. A stall is ruined if its structure is broken and it cannot trade. A stall is occupied if enemies are inside and controlling it. A stall is serving a customer when a customer reaches it, pays coins, and stock is reduced. If the customer panics first, the sale fails and the customer leaves. The game counts the market as open if a majority of its trade stalls are still functional and at least one customer is buying. The night ends when the dawn bell rings; if the market is still open, the player wins. The market is lost if a majority of stalls are ruined, closed, or occupied, or if the market cannot serve any customer for a full raid.
- market, stalls, and aisles: An aisle is a market path with a stall, stock, customers, and possible enemy routes. The market is open, fragile, smelly, and full of things that fail. Market objects and areas can be fresh, open, spoiled, blocked, slippery, noisy, ruined, repaired, or occupied. Fresh stalls trade normally. Spoiled stalls smell, attract pests, and repel customers. Blocked aisles slow enemies but may also trap cats. Slippery aisles make cats fall and slow everyone. Noisy aisles make cats twitchy and customers panic. Occupied stalls are controlled by enemies and cannot trade. Protected aisles have defenses or cats assigned and take less damage. Bait aisles have open fish, high-value stock, or strong smells and draw enemies. Rich aisles earn more coins. Safe aisles are clean, fresh, and calm, keeping customers willing to stay. Problematic aisles are spoiled, slippery, blocked, or noisy, making them hard to defend and easy to lose. A stall becomes taken over when an enemy reaches it and remains there after making the stall unsafe: a raccoon settles over an opened crate, a rival cat claims the counter, or gulls leave the stall so wrecked that it is under enemy control. Occupation lasts until the enemy is cleared. It can be cleared by a cat defeating or driving it out, by a bell or other loud disturbance startling it, or by the player giving up the aisle and closing the stall. After clearing, the stall is damaged. If it is repaired, it can trade again. If it is not repaired, it becomes ruined. Repair returns an object to a weaker version of its former state.
- customers: Customers enter through the market gate when the market is open and the gate is not blocked or occupied. They walk in, look for a fresh, clean, unoccupied stall, and decide whether to trade. If the stall is fresh and the aisle is clean, they buy and leave with coins. If the stall is spoiled or the aisle is noisy, they complain, which lowers their patience and can make them leave without buying. Complaints are caused by smells, noise, ruined stalls, and failed service; each complaint lowers their patience. If an enemy is in the aisle, a flung trout flies past, a stampede starts, or the noise of fighting reaches them, they panic, flee, and may knock things over on the way out. They leave when they have bought, when they are scared, or when the market closes. No customers means no coins and no win.
- stock and fish supply: The market begins the night with stock: crates of fresh fish behind the stalls and some already laid out under the lamps. As the night goes on, a dock cart drops a new crate at the loading rail at set points, and a cat can be ordered to haul it to a stall. Fish can also be reclaimed by a cat from a flung trout that lands in the market, or from a caught pest that was carrying one. If a stall runs out of stock, it cannot trade, earns no coins, and its empty smell and open counter become bait that draws pests and tempts cats off post. The player can move stock from one stall to another, but that takes a cat’s attention and leaves the first stall bare.
- resources, economy, and upkeep: The market has four things it can lose or spend: coins, fresh fish, ice, and scraps. Coins come from customers buying fish. Fresh fish is sold, stolen, eaten by cats, or spoiled. Ice keeps stalls fresh and melts away as it is used. Scraps come from broken crates, nets, fish bones, and waste. Building, repairs, hiring, feeding cats, and keeping stalls fresh all have costs. A working stall requires ice to keep fresh fish fresh, cleaning to keep smell and noise down, and repair to keep its counter, roof, and stock intact. A working defense requires durability and, if it depends on bait or weight, the materials that keep it set. A working cat requires food from fresh fish or scraps, rest, and not being overworked. The player pays these requirements by spending ice, scraps, coins, and orders: assign ice to a stall, order a repair, feed a cat, rest a cat, or close a stall to stop its upkeep. If an upkeep cannot be paid, the stall spoils, the cat ignores orders, or the defense fails.
- cat units, commands, and obedience: Cats are market cats obtained from the starting roster, hired as strays between raids, or returned if they are fed and kept safe. Starting cats are asleep in safe corners. The player can assign a sleeping cat to a post, but assigning it does not wake it. To wake it before the raid, the player must feed it, pay a scrap, or use a market noise like the bell; waking by feeding costs resources, while waking by noise may scare customers or leave cats nervous. If a cat is left asleep during the first raid, it does nothing, and if enemies reach it, it may be startled, flee, or cause noise that panics customers. The player may assign cats to zones, order them to chase, guard, return, rest, or repair, and use placed defenses and market objects during a raid. The player cannot directly attack enemies; cats and market objects do the work. Orders only work if the cat is present, awake, fed, not panicked, and not more strongly pulled by a habit or nearby fish. Assigning a cat sends it to a zone and holds it there. Guarding means it attacks the first enemy that enters that zone. Chasing means it follows one enemy until that enemy is caught, driven off, or leaves the market. Repairing means it spends time and scraps to restore a stall or defense. Resting means it cannot act but recovers obedience and condition. Fighting means it exchanges damage with whatever it can reach. A cat becomes injured when its condition fails and flees or hides; it is lost if it leaves the market or is not brought back. Feeding, resting, and cleaning repair cats. A lost cat flees, gets injured, or hides, and is unavailable until healed. Obedience falls when cats are hungry, frightened, or overworked, and rises when they are fed and successful. A cat naps on its own when it has been left in a calm zone with no threats for a while, or when it is overworked or hungry and not fed. It is not the player ordering rest; it is the cat falling asleep at its post. While napping, it ignores every order, does not fight or chase, and its assigned zone is open. It wakes when an enemy enters the zone, when the bell rings, when another cat disturbs it, or when the player feeds it. Obedience drops while it naps and drops further if it is woken by a scare.
- enemy raids and hazards: A raid begins when enemies cross into the market from the docks, alleys, or roofs. It ends when every enemy has fled, been driven off, been caught, or left with stolen goods. Enemies are hungry, fast, stupid, and opportunistic. If enemies pass through a gap, they move toward the nearest bait, stall, or stock. If they reach a protected thing, that thing is damaged, opened, stolen from, or spoiled. If they are not stopped, the target changes state and the enemy leaves with loot. An enemy is caught if it is small enough and defeated; it is driven off if fear or pain makes it flee; it leaves with loot if it reaches its target before being stopped. Enemies are stopped by cats, intact defenses, traps, nets, or by being driven off before reaching a target. Gulls target exposed fish, signs, and open lids; they dive, steal small fish, and spoil stalls if not driven off. Seagulls are a larger, bolder harbor variant that dive at stalls, knock customers, and carry off bigger fish. Raccoons target closed containers and barricades; they open lids, pull crates, and steal stock if not stopped. Rival cats target high-value stalls and market cats; they fight, steal fish, and lure defenders away. Dockworkers are hazards: they knock over crates, create spills, and make aisles slippery.
- basic market defenses and object durability: The player places market defenses into the market: netted awnings, ice boxes, fish-barrel barricades, hooks, ropes, and little traps made from market junk. Netted awnings block gulls. Ice boxes keep stalls fresh but melt. Fish-barrel barricades slow or block enemies but can be knocked over. Hooks and ropes snag enemies or trigger traps. Traps catch small pests but must be reset. A defense is weakened when its durability drops, broken when it reaches zero, reset by repair, and left as scrap if not repaired. Objects are real if intact and correctly placed, weak if damaged, broken if durability fails, repairable with scraps, and decorative if they only look useful but have no active effect.
- bell and emergency response: The fish-market bell is a market object the player can use during a raid. Ringing it wakes nearby cats, pulls their attention toward the sound, and can make them break post to respond. It also makes customers nervous and can cause complaints. The dawn bell is not a tool. It is the end-of-night signal and cannot be rung, delayed, or controlled by the player. The fish-market bell affects the market while the night is still happening; the dawn bell ends the night and decides whether the market survived.
supporting mechanics:
- advanced cat roles and patrol: A general alarm cat is not assigned to guard one stall. It is assigned to watch entrances, aisles, or the whole market, and when it sees enemies enter, it yowls. That yowl warns the player, wakes nearby cats, and makes them shift toward the threat, but it also makes customers nervous. Guarding a stall directly blocks enemies from that stall; an alarm cat trades direct defense for early warning and a wider response. Patrolling is a route assignment: the player draws a line through an aisle or across a row of stalls, and the cat walks that line, attacking anything that enters along it and covering several positions with one cat. The player uses it to hold a long stretch with fewer cats.
- bait, tempting fish, and cat-cat events: Tempting fish are physical bait items left in the market: a salmon, a fish head, spilled stock, or a high-value fish. They create a pull point that can draw cats or enemies. A cat breaks post if its hunger, curiosity, or habit is stronger than its assigned order, moves to the item, and may eat it, carry it, or leave it as bait for pests. Cat-cat events happen when two cats are assigned near each other or left together: they groom, follow one another, or block each other, making nearby orders less reliable and sometimes moving a cat without the player intending it. The player can use these deliberately by placing bait to lure enemies into traps, or prevent them by separating cats and feeding them.
- bad fish detection and removal: A bad fish is fish whose freshness has dropped into the spoiled state. It creates a smell that lowers customer patience, attracts pests, and can make a stall a source of complaints. A cat with the bad-fish habit detects it before a customer reacts: she yowls, marks the bad stock, and gives the player a chance to isolate, remove, repair, or dump it. Other cats can move or remove it if ordered, but doing so may leave their post open.
- flung trout events: The flying trout is not an enemy and not a separate object the player controls. It is a “flung fish” event: a gull, raccoon, startled customer, or knocked-over crate sends a fish through the air. When it flies past a customer, the customer backs away and loses patience; when it lands, it leaves a wet, slippery patch and may distract nearby cats. A cat can retrieve it as fresh fish, but only by leaving its post.
- advanced market objects: Net launchers stun flying enemies. Eel barrels make aisles slippery and can be used to slip enemies into traps. Salted herring barrels are preserved stock and strong bait. They are safer than fresh fish because they spoil slowly, and they can be sold for good coins if the market stays open. But open barrels create a strong smell that draws gulls, raccoons, rival cats, and hungry market cats. Closed barrels are safer but cannot be sold; opened barrels can be used to lure pests into defended aisles or traps, and if ruined they become a smell hazard that repels customers. The broken scale measures the weight and freshness of fish at a stall. When it works, it gives true values and helps the player know what can still be sold. When it gives false readings, customers may be sent to spoiled stock, bad fish may be missed, and coins become unreliable. When it is broken, it is only a prop. If repaired with scraps, it becomes accurate again and helps the player avoid selling bad fish.
- progression and strange raids: Raids get stranger when the night progresses and when the market state makes new possibilities. At higher waves, enemies combine tactics: gulls distract while raccoons open lids; rival cats lure defenders away from occupied stalls; dockworkers create spills that make aisles slippery; nervous customers panic and stampede, opening gaps. Market problems begin from failed upkeep and enemy actions: melted ice, spoiled fish, broken nets, slippery aisles, and strong smells. The player’s own neglected stalls and broken defenses are what make the later raids feel different.
- cascading failure and forced sacrifice: Failures cascade through linked states: a spoiled stall makes customers leave, less coin means fewer repairs, more broken stalls mean more enemies slip through, and hungry cats obey worse. A cat abandoning post opens a gap, which lets enemies take stock, which lowers fresh fish, which makes more cats distractible. The player can survive by choosing which failure to absorb: close a ruined aisle, let bait be stolen, sacrifice a poor stall, or move cats from one danger to another.
- joke behavior rules: The jokes are playable behavior rules, not just flavor. A cat grooming itself during a raid means it is distracted and will ignore orders for a short time. A gull stealing a “No Gulls” sign is an enemy action that removes a deterrent and makes the area more tempting. Customers complaining are a state change: repeated complaints lower patience and can turn into panic, which damages the market.
extra mechanics:
- market legibility and mastery: After the tenth minute, the game becomes about knowing the market as a living thing. The player starts to see which cats will obey, which aisles are bait, which defenses are only for show, and which enemies will try which weakness. They choose what to let fail. Do they trade a safe aisle for a richer one? Do they spend on ice, on stronger defenses, on a better cat, or on a quick repair? Do they keep a cat as a general alarm or send her to guard the smoked eels? The market changes as stalls are repaired, blocked, ruined, or taken over. The raids get stranger. The player is not just surviving; they are keeping a working place alive.
