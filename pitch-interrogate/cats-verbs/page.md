# The pitcher's page

Ask: `a tower defense game where cats defend a fish market`

**Page for myself**

The game is made of waves, combat, economy, building, units, management, upkeep, multitasking.

I see a wet dockside fish market at night, all lamplight, ice, fish scales, and hungry animals. The player is the market’s head cat: a scarred, practical tabby who has to keep the market trading through the night. This is not a game where the player is one small cat running around with health or needs. The player is the market’s keeper, the one who decides where the cats go and how the market holds together.

The first minutes are the market waking up and getting attacked. The player sees the stalls, the crates, the aisles, the sleeping cats, the fish glistening under the lamps. They start putting defenses into the market: netted awnings, ice boxes, fish-barrel barricades, hooks, ropes, little traps made from market junk. They tell the cats where to watch, where to chase, where to guard. The first raid comes in as a squawk of gulls, a scrabbling of raccoons, a nervous customer backing away from a flying trout. The cats move. The defenses take hits. The market stays open.

What works against them is the market itself. It is open, fragile, smelly, and full of things that fail. Ice melts. Fish spoils. Cats get distracted by scraps, naps, other cats, or a particularly tempting salmon. Every aisle has something to protect and something that can turn into a problem. The enemies are not just attackers; they are hungry, fast, stupid, and opportunistic. Gulls dive. Raccoons open lids. Cats from rival markets steal fish. Dockworkers knock things over. The player’s own cats are not clean, obedient machines; they can be drawn off post by a fish head.

When it goes wrong, the market stops being funny and starts being dangerous. A cat abandons its post. A stall’s ice fails. Fish spoils. A wave slips through a gap. A customer panics. A stall is ruined. The player loses what little they have, and the next problem comes in faster. The place becomes slippery, noisy, and hard to read. Cats scatter. The player is forced to save one part of the market by letting another part fail.

After the tenth minute, the game becomes about knowing the market as a living thing. The player starts to see which cats will obey, which aisles are bait, which defenses are only for show, and which enemies will try which weakness. They choose what to let fail. Do they trade a safe aisle for a richer one? Do they spend on ice, on stronger defenses, on a better cat, or on a quick repair? Do they keep a cat as a general alarm or send her to guard the smoked eels? The market changes as stalls are repaired, blocked, ruined, or taken over. The raids get stranger. The player is not just surviving; they are keeping a working place alive.

The particular things in it: cats with names and habits. Mackerel Mouth, a one-eared cat who ignores gulls but hates seagulls. Boots, who patrols well but likes to nap. Whisker, who can smell a bad fish before a customer does. Objects like salted herring barrels, ice crates, fish-barrel barricades, net launchers, fish hooks, eel barrels, a broken scale that still sort of works, and a fish-market bell. Jokes: a cat grooming itself during a raid, a gull stealing a “No Gulls” sign, customers complaining about the smell while the market is being attacked.

This game is not a platformer, not a stealth game, not a one-cat survival game, not a story RPG, not a farm game, not a racing game, not a game about moving one cat through rooftops. It is not about the player being a single cat with health and needs. The player runs and commands the market. It is not a generic medieval tower defense with wizards and castles. It is a wet, smelly, funny fish market defended by cats.

**Pitch**

You are the head cat of a smelly dockside fish market. You keep the market open while gulls, raccoons, and rival cats raid it: you set defenses, tell your cats where to watch, and use whatever the market makes to keep it from falling apart. The game is a tower defense game where the market is alive and messy—ice melts, fish spoils, cats get distracted, and every aisle you ignore starts costing you. The goal is not to win a battle on a clean path, but to keep a working place alive through the night.
