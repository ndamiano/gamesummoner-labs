## Round 1

### Interrogator

CRACKS

8. The page says the market itself works against the player and changes through melting, spoiling, ruin, repair, blockage, takeover, and becoming “slippery, noisy, and hard to read,” but does not define those states or their effects.

QUESTIONS
1. “The goal is not to win a battle on a clean path, but to keep a working place alive through the night.” What does the game count as keeping the market open, and what conditions end the night as success or failure?

2. “The game is made of waves, combat, economy, building, units, management, upkeep, multitasking.” What is the basic time structure: what may happen continuously, what may happen during a raid, and what may happen between raids?

3. “The first raid comes in” and “A wave slips through a gap.” What begins and ends a raid, and what happens when enemies pass through, reach a protected thing, or are not stopped?

5. “you set defenses, tell your cats where to watch” and “They tell the cats where to watch, where to chase, where to guard.” What actions are permitted to the player, what can they change or command, and what rules decide whether those interventions work?

6. “cats with names and habits” and “The player starts to see which cats will obey” and “Do they keep a cat as a general alarm or send her to guard the smoked eels?” How do cats’ habits, roles, obedience, distraction, naps, learning, and possible loss actually work in the rules?

13. “The player loses what little they have, and the next problem comes in faster.” and “The player is forced to save one part of the market by letting another part fail.” How do failures combine, escalate, or become tradeoffs the player must choose?

14. “Jokes: a cat grooming itself during a raid, a gull stealing a “No Gulls” sign, customers complaining about the smell while the market is being attacked.” What do these jokes mean in play: flavor, random events, behavior rules, enemy actions, customer actions, or something else?

### Pitcher

1. The game counts the market as open if a majority of its trade stalls are still functional and at least one customer is buying. The night ends when the dawn bell rings; if the market is still open, the player wins. The market is lost if a majority of stalls are ruined, closed, or occupied, or if the market cannot serve any customer for a full raid.

2. Upkeep, spoilage, cat behavior, and customer patience run continuously. During a raid, enemies enter, cats fight or chase, objects can break, and the player may issue emergency orders, ring the bell, or use placed defenses. Between raids, the player may build, repair, sell fish, spend coins, reassign cats, and choose what to sacrifice before the next wave.

3. A raid begins when enemies cross into the market from the docks, alleys, or roofs. It ends when every enemy has fled, been driven off, been caught, or left with stolen goods. If enemies pass through a gap, they move toward the nearest bait, stall, or stock; if they reach a protected thing, that thing is damaged, opened, stolen from, or spoiled; if they are not stopped, the target changes state and the enemy leaves with loot.

4. The market has four things it can lose or spend: coins, fresh fish, ice, and scraps. Coins come from customers buying fish; fresh fish is sold, stolen, eaten by cats, or spoiled; ice keeps stalls fresh and melts into usable water; scraps come from broken crates, nets, fish bones, and waste. Building, repairs, hiring, feeding cats, and keeping stalls fresh all have costs. If an upkeep cannot be paid, the stall spoils, the cat ignores orders, or the defense fails.

5. The player may assign cats to zones, order them to chase, guard, return, rest, or repair, and use placed defenses and market objects during a raid. The player cannot directly attack enemies; cats and market objects do the work. Orders only work if the cat is present, awake, fed, not panicked, and not more strongly pulled by a habit or nearby fish.

6. Cats are market cats obtained from the starting roster, hired as strays between raids, or returned if they are fed and kept safe. A lost cat flees, gets injured, or hides, and is unavailable until healed. Feeding, resting, and cleaning repair them. Each cat has habits: Mackerel Mouth may ignore other enemies but fight gulls, Boots may obey well but nap, and a cat that smells a bad fish may break post to inspect it. Obedience falls when cats are hungry, frightened, or overworked, and rises when they are fed and successful.

7. Gulls target exposed fish, signs, and open lids; they dive, steal small fish, and spoil stalls if not driven off. Raccoons target closed containers and barricades; they open lids, pull crates, and steal stock if not stopped. Rival cats target high-value stalls and market cats; they fight, steal fish, and lure defenders away. Dockworkers are hazards: they knock over crates, create spills, and make aisles slippery. Enemies are stopped by cats, intact defenses, traps, nets, or by being driven off before reaching a target.

8. Market objects and areas can be fresh, open, spoiled, blocked, slippery, noisy, ruined, repaired, or occupied. Fresh stalls trade normally. Spoiled stalls smell, attract pests, and repel customers. Blocked aisles slow enemies but may also trap cats. Slippery aisles make cats fall and slow everyone. Noisy aisles make cats twitchy and customers panic. Occupied stalls are controlled by enemies and cannot trade. Repair returns an object to a weaker version of its former state.

9. Customers enter, choose fresh stalls, buy fish, and leave. Their purchases make coins. Complaints are caused by smells, noise, ruined stalls, and failed service; each complaint lowers their patience. If patience fails, they panic, flee, knock things over, or stampede, which can damage stalls and make the market harder to keep open. No customers means no coins and no win.

10. An aisle is a market path with a stall, stock, customers, and possible enemy routes. Protected aisles have defenses or cats assigned and take less damage. Bait aisles have open fish, high-value stock, or strong smells and draw enemies. Rich aisles earn more coins. Safe aisles are clean, fresh, and calm, keeping customers willing to stay. Problematic aisles are spoiled, slippery, blocked, or noisy, making them hard to defend and easy to lose.

11. Netted awnings block gulls. Ice boxes keep stalls fresh but melt. Fish-barrel barricades slow or block enemies but can be knocked over. Hooks and ropes snag enemies or trigger traps. Traps catch small pests but must be reset. Net launchers stun flying enemies. Eel barrels make aisles slippery and can be used to slip enemies into traps. The broken scale gives false readings unless repaired. The bell can rally cats or scare customers, depending on timing. Objects are real if intact and correctly placed, weak if damaged, broken if durability fails, repairable with scraps, and decorative if they only look useful but have no active effect.

12. Before the first raid, the market is waking: the player may place initial defenses, assign starting cats, and choose the first stall to trade. The first raid begins when the market opens and customers arrive. As the night progresses, upkeep costs rise, ice melts faster, enemies bring new tactics, stalls become more fragile, customers become more nervous, and random market problems appear more often.

13. Failures cascade through linked states: a spoiled stall makes customers leave, less coin means fewer repairs, more broken stalls mean more enemies slip through, and hungry cats obey worse. A cat abandoning post opens a gap, which lets enemies take stock, which lowers fresh fish, which makes more cats distractible. The player can survive by choosing which failure to absorb: close a ruined aisle, let bait be stolen, sacrifice a poor stall, or move cats from one danger to another.

14. The jokes are playable behavior rules, not just flavor. A cat grooming itself during a raid means it is distracted and will ignore orders for a short time. A gull stealing a “No Gulls” sign is an enemy action that removes a deterrent and makes the area more tempting. Customers complaining are a state change: repeated complaints lower patience and can turn into panic, which damages the market.

## Round 2

### Interrogator

CRACKS
- The page names a cat behavior that treats gulls and seagulls as different: “Mackerel Mouth, a one-eared cat who ignores gulls but hates seagulls.” It has not ruled what seagulls are, whether they differ from gulls, or what the cat actually does.

QUESTIONS
1. “Mackerel Mouth, a one-eared cat who ignores gulls but hates seagulls.” What are gulls and seagulls in this game, and what does Mackerel Mouth do to each?

2. “The first raid comes in as a squawk of gulls, a scrabbling of raccoons, a nervous customer backing away from a flying trout.” What is the flying trout in play, how does it come into the market, and what does it cause?

3. “Objects like salted herring barrels.” What do salted herring barrels do, and how do they affect cats, enemies, customers, or the market?

4. “The player sees the stalls, the crates, the aisles, the sleeping cats, the fish glistening under the lamps.” What rules decide whether a sleeping cat can be assigned, woken, or left asleep, and what happens in each case?

5. “Do they keep a cat as a general alarm or send her to guard the smoked eels?” What does a general alarm cat do, and how is that different from guarding a stall?

### Pitcher

1. In this game, “gulls” are the small dock pests that hop, squawk, snatch scraps, and open lids. “Seagulls” are a larger, bolder harbor variant that dive at stalls, knock customers, and carry off bigger fish. Mackerel Mouth ignores gulls: she will not chase them, attack them, or be pulled off post by them, even if they are eating fish. But if a seagull appears, she breaks orders and attacks it first, even if that leaves her assigned stall open.

2. The flying trout is not an enemy and not a separate object the player controls. It is a “flung fish” event: a gull, raccoon, startled customer, or knocked-over crate sends a fish through the air. When it flies past a customer, the customer backs away and loses patience; when it lands, it leaves a wet, slippery patch and may distract nearby cats. A cat can retrieve it as fresh fish, but only by leaving its post.

3. Salted herring barrels are preserved stock and strong bait. They are safer than fresh fish because they spoil slowly, and they can be sold for good coins if the market stays open. But open barrels create a strong smell that draws gulls, raccoons, rival cats, and hungry market cats. Closed barrels are safer but cannot be sold; opened barrels can be used to lure pests into defended aisles or traps, and if ruined they become a smell hazard that repels customers.

4. Starting cats are asleep in safe corners. The player can assign a sleeping cat to a post, but assigning it does not wake it. To wake it before the raid, the player must feed it, pay a scrap, or use a market noise like the bell; waking by feeding costs resources, while waking by noise may scare customers or leave cats nervous. If a cat is left asleep during the first raid, it does nothing, and if enemies reach it, it may be startled, flee, or cause noise that panics customers.

5. A general alarm cat is not assigned to guard one stall. It is assigned to watch entrances, aisles, or the whole market, and when it sees enemies enter, it yowls. That yowl warns the player, wakes nearby cats, and makes them shift toward the threat, but it also makes customers nervous. Guarding a stall directly blocks enemies from that stall; an alarm cat trades direct defense for early warning and a wider response.

8. “a broken scale that still sort of works.” What does the scale do when it is used, and what changes when it gives false readings, is broken, or is repaired?

9. “cats get distracted by scraps, naps, other cats, or a particularly tempting salmon” and “drawn off post by a fish head.” What are the tempting fish, cat-cat events, or fish-head events, and how do they change a cat’s post, orders, or the market?

### Pitcher

1. A cat is a unit with its own position, condition, and obedience. Assigning it sends it to a zone and holds it there. Guarding means it attacks the first enemy that enters that zone. Chasing means it follows one enemy until that enemy is caught, driven off, or leaves the market. Repairing means it spends time and scraps to restore a stall or defense. Resting means it cannot act but recovers obedience and condition. Fighting means it exchanges damage with whatever it can reach. An enemy is caught if it is small enough and defeated; it is driven off if fear or pain makes it flee; it leaves with loot if it reaches its target before being stopped. A cat becomes injured when its condition fails and flees or hides; it is lost if it leaves the market or is not brought back. A defense is weakened when its durability drops, broken when it reaches zero, reset by repair, and left as scrap if not repaired.

2. The fish-market bell is a market object the player can use during a raid. Ringing it wakes nearby cats, pulls their attention toward the sound, and can make them break post to respond. It also makes customers nervous and can cause complaints. The dawn bell is not a tool. It is the end-of-night signal and cannot be rung, delayed, or controlled by the player. The fish-market bell affects the market while the night is still happening; the dawn bell ends the night and decides whether the market survived.

3. A stall is open if it is not blocked, not occupied, and has stock available. It is functional if its stock is fresh enough to sell, its counter is intact, and the aisle is not so spoiled, noisy, or slippery that customers refuse to trade. A stall is closed if the player shuts it or it has no sellable stock. A stall is ruined if its structure is broken and it cannot trade. A stall is occupied if enemies are inside and controlling it. A stall is serving a customer when a customer reaches it, pays coins, and stock is reduced. If the customer panics first, the sale fails and the customer leaves.

4. A stall becomes taken over when an enemy reaches it and remains there after making the stall unsafe: a raccoon settles over an opened crate, a rival cat claims the counter, or gulls leave the stall so wrecked that it is under enemy control. Occupation lasts until the enemy is cleared. It can be cleared by a cat defeating or driving it out, by a bell or other loud disturbance startling it, or by the player giving up the aisle and closing the stall. After clearing, the stall is damaged. If it is repaired, it can trade again. If it is not repaired, it becomes ruined.

5. A working stall requires ice to keep fresh fish fresh, cleaning to keep smell and noise down, and repair to keep its counter, roof, and stock intact. A working defense requires durability and, if it depends on bait or weight, the materials that keep it set. A working cat requires food from fresh fish or scraps, rest, and not being overworked. The player pays these requirements by spending ice, scraps, coins, and orders: assign ice to a stall, order a repair, feed a cat, rest a cat, or close a stall to stop its upkeep. If a requirement is missed, the stall spoils, the cat ignores orders, or the defense fails.

6. Raids get stranger when the night progresses and when the market state makes new possibilities. At higher waves, enemies combine tactics: gulls distract while raccoons open lids; rival cats lure defenders away from occupied stalls; dockworkers create spills that make aisles slippery; nervous customers panic and stampede, opening gaps. Market problems begin from failed upkeep and enemy actions: melted ice, spoiled fish, broken nets, slippery aisles, and strong smells. The player’s own neglected stalls and broken defenses are what make the later raids feel different.

7. A bad fish is fish whose freshness has dropped into the spoiled state. It creates a smell that lowers customer patience, attracts pests, and can make a stall a source of complaints. Whisker detects it before a customer reacts: she yowls, marks the bad stock, and gives the player a chance to isolate, remove, repair, or dump it. Other cats can move or remove it if ordered, but doing so may leave their post open.

8. The scale measures the weight and freshness of fish at a stall. When it works, it gives true values and helps the player know what can still be sold. When it gives false readings, customers may be sent to spoiled stock, bad fish may be missed, and coins become unreliable. When it is broken, it is only a prop. If repaired with scraps, it becomes accurate again and helps the player avoid selling bad fish.

9. Tempting fish are physical bait items left in the market: a salmon, a fish head, spilled stock, or a high-value fish. They create a pull point that can draw cats or enemies. A cat breaks post if its hunger, curiosity, or habit is stronger than its assigned order, moves to the item, and may eat it, carry it, or leave it as bait for pests. Cat-cat events happen when two cats are assigned near each other or left together: they groom, follow one another, or block each other, making nearby orders less reliable and sometimes moving a cat without the player intending it. The player can use these deliberately by placing bait to lure enemies into traps, or prevent them by separating cats and feeding them.

## Round 4

### Interrogator

CRACKS
- The page makes fresh fish and trading central, but it never says how the market gets fresh fish into its stalls or what happens when a stall has no stock. This leaves the core economy and the rule for a stall being open without a source.
- The page makes customers necessary for trading, coins, complaints, panic, and loss, but it never says what causes customers to enter the market or what decides whether they will trade, complain, panic, or leave. This leaves the win condition and the customer system without a trigger.
- The page says cats get distracted by naps, but it does not say when a cat takes a nap, what wakes it, or how that changes its post, orders, and obedience. This leaves a named messy behavior unresolved.
- The page names Boots as a cat who “patrols well but likes to nap,” but it does not say what patrolling is as a playable action, what the player can do with Boots, or how his napping habit differs from ordinary rest. This leaves a named cat without a place in the order of things.

QUESTIONS
1. “The player is the market’s head cat: … has to keep the market trading through the night.” “use whatever the market makes to keep it from falling apart.” How does the market get fresh fish into its stalls, and what happens when a stall cannot stock or runs out of stock?

2. “keep the market trading through the night.” “A customer panics.” What causes customers to enter the market, and what decides whether they will trade, complain, panic, or leave?

3. “Cats get distracted by scraps, naps, other cats, or a particularly tempting salmon.” What makes a cat take a nap or be pulled away by a nap, and what happens to its post, orders, and obedience while it naps?

4. “Boots, who patrols well but likes to nap.” What is “patrolling” in play, what can the player do with Boots, and how does his napping habit work?

### Pitcher

1. The market begins the night with stock: crates of fresh fish behind the stalls and some already laid out under the lamps. As the night goes on, a dock cart drops a new crate at the loading rail at set points, and a cat can be ordered to haul it to a stall. Fish can also be reclaimed by a cat from a flung trout that lands in the market, or from a caught pest that was carrying one. If a stall runs out of stock, it cannot trade, earns no coins, and its empty smell and open counter become bait that draws pests and tempts cats off post. The player can move stock from one stall to another, but that takes a cat's attention and leaves the first stall bare.

2. Customers enter through the market gate when the market is open and the gate is not blocked or occupied. They walk in, look for a fresh, clean, unoccupied stall, and decide whether to trade. If the stall is fresh and the aisle is clean, they buy and leave with coins. If the stall is spoiled or the aisle is noisy, they complain, which lowers their patience and can make them leave without buying. If an enemy is in the aisle, a flung trout flies past, a stampede starts, or the noise of fighting reaches them, they panic, flee, and may knock things over on the way out. They leave when they have bought, when they are scared, or when the market closes.

3. A cat naps on its own when it has been left in a calm zone with no threats for a while, or when it is overworked or hungry and not fed. It is not the player ordering rest; it is the cat falling asleep at its post. While napping, it ignores every order, does not fight or chase, and its assigned zone is open. It wakes when an enemy enters the zone, when the bell rings, when another cat disturbs it, or when the player feeds it. Obedience drops while it naps and drops further if it is woken by a scare.

4. Patrolling is a route assignment: the player draws a line through an aisle or across a row of stalls, and Boots walks that line, attacking anything that enters along it and covering several positions with one cat. The player uses him to hold a long stretch with fewer cats. His napping habit is that after he finishes a patrol loop with nothing to fight, he sits down and sleeps at the end of the route, leaving the far end of the line uncovered until he is woken. This is not the player ordering rest; it is Boots doing it on his own, and it creates a gap the player has to cover, feed him through, or accept.

### Interrogator

INTERVIEW OVER
