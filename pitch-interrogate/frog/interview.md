# The interview

## Round 1

### Interrogator

THE GAME SO FAR
This is a platformer where the player controls Mire, a mud-stained tree frog trying to get home to a hidden marsh after the town drains the ponds. The game is presented as a small animal moving through a human-sized world: backyards, rooftops, and flooded gutters. Mire moves by running, leaping, and clinging, and uses the tongue to swing from wires, snag keys, and pull loose planks aside. The game is explicitly not about fighting or collecting shiny things; it is about crossing through dangerous spaces without being caught.

SETTLED
- It is a platformer where you play as a frog.
- You play as Mire, a mud-stained tree frog.
- Mire is trying to get home to the hidden marsh after the town drains the ponds.
- You run, leap, and cling through backyards, rooftops, and flooded gutters.
- You use your tongue to swing from wires, snag keys, and pull loose planks aside.
- This isn’t a frog that fights or collects shiny things.
- It’s a small animal moving through a human-sized world.
- Every crossing is about how to get across without being caught.

QUESTIONS
1. What does “being caught” mean in play? My guess is that getting caught by humans, pets, vehicles, or other threats does not kill Mire but ends the current crossing and makes you restart or lose progress.

2. Are keys, wires, and loose planks physical traversal tools that create the puzzle? My guess is that keys unlock gates or doors, wires are swing points, and loose planks block or redirect paths, making the tongue the main tool for solving each crossing.

3. What kinds of threats make Mire avoid being caught? My guess is that the threats are human-scale things like people, pets, traffic, sprinklers, or falling objects, not enemies with health bars or combat rules.

4. How does a run begin and end? My guess is that each crossing begins in one human space and ends in a safer space, and the game gets harder by making routes more exposed, timed, and dependent on the tongue’s interactions.

5. Which parts must work on the first day for this to feel like the right game? My guess is that run, leap, cling, and tongue use are essential from the start, while keys and planks may appear as early route tools rather than late-game extras.

### Pitcher

1. Yes. “Being caught” is a failure state, not a death state: if Mire is grabbed by a hand, herded by a dog, flushed into a drain, or penned into a bucket, the current crossing fails and she is returned to the last safe perch. The punishment is lost progress and lost time, not lives.

2. Yes, but they are not collectible items. Wires are anchor points for tongue-swinging, loose planks are physical objects that can be pulled, nudged, or used to form temporary bridges, and keys are latch objects that can be snagged and pulled out or pushed into a gate catch to open a way. The tongue is the main tool for solving the crossing.

3. Exactly. The threats are ordinary human-scale dangers: people, cats, lawnmowers, sprinklers, traffic, open drains, and things that move when Mire enters their space. They are hazards with behavior, not enemies with health bars or combat rules.

4. Yes. Each level is a single crossing: start from a safe wet perch, cross a human space, and reach the next safe wet perch. The game gets harder by making the route more exposed, more timed, and more dependent on tongue-based interactions like swing, pull, release, and anchor placement.

5. Yes. The first playable slice must nail run, leap, cling, tongue grab, tongue swing, tongue pull, and being caught. Keys and planks can come after that, but the tongue must be usable for basic traversal immediately; without it, the game is just a frog with a costume on.

QUESTIONS
1. How is the tongue limited so it does not solve every crossing instantly? My guess is that the tongue has no meter, but it is limited by reach, line of sight, and only works on clearly marked sturdy anchors; if Mire chooses the wrong anchor or mistimes a release, she becomes exposed instead of being instantly punished by a separate failure mechanic.

2. What exactly counts as “being caught”? My guess is that Mire is only caught when she enters an active hazard’s path or is grabbed, herded, flushed, or penned by it, and that ordinary falling from a ledge does not by itself count as being caught unless the landing place is itself a hazard such as a drain, bucket, sprinkler, hand, or moving object.

3. Are safe wet perches only the start and end of a crossing, or can there be intermediate safe perches? My guess is that there are no ordinary checkpoints inside a crossing: the start perch is the only safe spot until the end perch, and if intermediate perches exist they must be rare, clearly wet, and only appear as deliberate safety islands in very long crossings.

4. Does a failed crossing reset all puzzle state? My guess is that yes, when Mire is caught, any pulled plank, moved key, opened gate, or changed object resets to its starting state, so every crossing is a repeatable puzzle rather than a puzzle whose solution can be stored across failures.

5. How do hazards communicate danger before catching Mire? My guess is that hazards are telegraphed through motion, sound, water, vibration, or visible behavior, and that “being caught” is contact-based or timing-based rather than a separate stealth system; the danger comes from Mire choosing when to cross, not from hiding from a detection meter.

### Pitcher

The tongue is limited by reach, line of sight, and whether the anchor is something a real frog could stick to. There is no stamina meter or cooldown, but it cannot pass through walls, glass, mesh, or other barriers, and weak anchors can slip if Mire grabs at the wrong angle or with too much momentum. A bad grab does not trigger a separate punishment; it simply leaves her hanging, slipping, or exposed.

Caught means a hazard actually captures her: a hand closes over her, a dog herds her into a bucket, a drain current pulls her down, a sprinkler sweeps her into a wall, or a mower pushes her into a grate. Falling off a normal ledge is not caught by itself; it just changes where she lands. If that landing place is itself part of an active hazard, then the hazard catches her, not the fall.

The start perch is the only guaranteed safe place. Intermediate safe perches are rare, deliberate, and visibly wet or hidden, like a damp pot, a rain trap in a gutter, or the shaded underside of a planter that hazards cannot reach. They are not ordinary ledges or cover points; they are safety islands, and if they appear they also become the last perch Mire returns to if caught later.

A failed crossing resets the crossing’s puzzle state. A pulled plank snaps back, a key returns to its latch, an opened gate closes, and anything Mire shifted through the tongue returns to where it began. If she has reached a rare intermediate safe perch, the reset starts there and resets everything beyond it, so the crossing remains a clean repeatable puzzle rather than a sequence of permanent changes.

Hazards announce themselves through the world: a cat crouches and fixes its eyes before pouncing, a sprinkler sweeps in a clean arc, a drain gurgles before the current rises, a mower drones closer along a fixed path, a hand reaches in after a door opens. There is no stealth meter or detection bar; the danger comes from Mire reading the behavior and choosing the moment, anchor, and route that avoids it.

5. Yes. The tongue only affects environmental objects—wires, keys, planks, latches, loose boards, and other things that can be moved or anchored; Mire cannot grab a cat, push a person, stop a mower, or otherwise alter a hazard itself.
