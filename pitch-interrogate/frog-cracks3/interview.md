# The interview

## Round 1

### Interrogator

CRACKS
- The page gives a visible final destination but no rule for completing the game or what happens when the frog reaches the pond.
- The page says there are “levels” and “crafted places” and an “opening phase,” but no rule for what starts one, ends one, or marks the end of teaching.
- The tongue is the main verb, but the rules for what it can catch, what makes an object weak, and what happens when it catches insects are not ruled.
- The tongue-swing is named, but the rules for swinging, releasing, and failing are not ruled.
- The tongue is said to “grab, yank, and pull,” but the rules for yanking/pulling and their physical consequences are not ruled.
- Wall sticking, sliding upward, and climbing are named, but the rules for when the frog can stick, slide, climb, or fall are not ruled.
- The cat’s noticing, approaching, swatting, and resetting the frog are named, but no rules for those states are ruled.
- The heron’s shadow, patrolling, striking, distraction, and release are named, but no rules for those states are ruled.
- Sprinklers are described as both starting “without warning” and being something the player can “start,” with no rule for activation, duration, or effect.
- Water, swimming, currents, edges, and fish snapping are named, but no rules for movement, failure, or fish behavior are ruled.
- Rain barrels tipping, leaves sinking, and drainpipes gurgling/spitting are named, but no rules for what triggers them or what they do are ruled.
- Wet, slick, sticky, brittle, moving, and wind-affected surfaces are named, but no rules for how they behave are ruled.
- Eating insects may cause hops, sneezes, dizziness, or hiccups, but the page says this is “not progress,” leaving whether these effects are usable mechanics unclear.
- “Last safe perch” is used as a reset rule, but no rule defines what a safe perch is or how return happens.
- The frog’s bulging eyes and nervous throat are named as particular features, but no rule says what they do in the game.

QUESTIONS
1. The page says “The goal is always visible in a distant shape: a rooftop pond, a ring of lilies, a faint chorus.” What does the player do to complete the game, and what happens when the frog reaches the pond?

2. The page says “After the opening phase, the game stops teaching and starts trusting. The levels become more vertical and more connected.” and “It is made of clear, crafted places.” What starts and ends a place or level, what marks the end of the opening phase, and how does the player move from one place to the next?

3. The page says “The tongue is the main verb, but it is not a magic rope. It catches things that are small, grabbable, and visible” and “If they guess slightly wrong, the tongue catches a weak object, and the object breaks or moves unexpectedly.” What rules decide whether the tongue catches, holds, slips, breaks, or moves an object, and what happens when it catches an insect?

4. The page says “The player holds the tongue control and the frog’s tongue extends, catching the branch. Releasing lets the frog swing. If the timing is wrong, the frog drops into a puddle.” What rules decide how the frog swings, how its arc changes, how it releases or detaches, and what happens if it fails?

5. The page says the tongue “can grab, yank, and pull” and gives “pull a cork from a pipe so water begins to flow and carries a leaf into place.” What rules decide what can be yanked or pulled, and what physical consequence follows when it is?

6. The page says “When the frog lands on it, its sticky pads catch. The player can slide upward against it.” and lists “stick, slide, tongue-grab, swing, climb.” What rules decide whether the frog sticks, slides upward, slides down, loses grip, or climbs when it touches a wall or similar surface?

7. The page says “Moving too much makes the cat step closer,” “A cat can swat at the frog if it is noticed,” and “the frog is reset to the last safe perch.” What rules decide when the cat notices the frog, how it approaches, when it swats, and how the reset happens?

8. The page says “A heron’s shadow passes over open ground,” “a heron patrols that pond, so the player may need to wait for a shadow to pass or disturb a nearby branch so the heron looks elsewhere,” and “the frog is coughed up nearby.” What rules decide what open ground is, when the heron notices or strikes, how it can be distracted, and how it releases the frog?

9. The page says “A sprinkler can start without warning, turning a ledge into a slide.” and “start a sprinkler to knock a caterpillar off a branch so the branch dips lower.” What rules decide when a sprinkler starts or stops, what it does to surfaces and objects, and what can make it happen?

10. The page says “Water carries the player in currents but also slows them down,” “If the frog gets into water, it can swim, but poorly, and it must haul itself onto the nearest edge,” and lists “a fish that snaps.” What rules decide how the frog moves through water, how currents carry or slow it, how it exits, and what the fish does?

11. The page says “Rain barrels tip. Leaves sink. Drainpipes gurgle and spit.” What rules decide when these objects tip, sink, gurgle, or spit, and how those changes affect the frog and the route?

12. The page says “Surfaces are wet, slick, sticky, brittle, or moving,” “a crumbling ledge,” “condensation makes the metal frames slippery,” and “wind that changes the arc of a hop.” What rules decide how slippery, sticky, brittle, moving, and wind-affected surfaces behave?

13. The page says “eat a wasp and get dizzy, causing a weird but useful sideways hop. Eating a fly might make it sneeze.” and “These are flavor and feedback, not progress.” What rules decide what happens when the frog eats an insect, and can the player intentionally use those effects?

14. The page says “the frog is reset to the last safe perch.” What rule defines a “safe perch,” and how does the frog get back to it?

15. The page says the frog has “bulging eyes that follow threats” and “a throat that swells when it is nervous.” What do these body features do in the game?

### Pitcher

1. The game completes as an arrival, not a trigger. Each place has an exit perch, but the whole game has one true exit: the final rooftop pond. When the frog lands in the lily ring and stays still for a breath, the other frogs stop, then the frog calls, and the game ends. Any earlier pond is a hazard or false goal, not completion.

2. A place is a bounded traversal from a wake perch to an exit perch. It starts when the frog settles on the wake perch; it ends when the frog touches and holds the exit perch, which slides the next place into view. The opening phase ends when the frog reaches the first place where the pond is fully visible and all core verbs have been used in one sequence; after that, no new verbs are taught, only harder and more connected places.

3. The tongue catches if the aim hits a visible grabbable thing: fibrous, hooked, corked, wire, leaf vein, spider silk, or an insect. It holds if the object can bear weight and the angle is not wrong; it slips on smooth wet surfaces; it breaks if weak—cracked, dry, frayed, too thin—or yanked too hard; it moves if loose or hanging. Insects are caught instantly and swallowed; they are not anchors and do not remain held, but they trigger the eating reaction.

4. Once attached, the frog swings as a pendulum from the anchor. The arc is set by the anchor, rope length, and momentum; crouching shortens and tenses the swing, and releasing or hopping adds a small launch. The player detaches by releasing the tongue action, and the frog leaves along its current velocity. If the anchor slips or breaks, the tongue detaches and the frog falls; if release timing is wrong, normal physics takes over.

5. Yank and pull are the same tongue action used against an object. The frog can pull anything the tongue can hold: loose objects drag toward the frog, fixed anchors pull the frog toward them, and mechanisms move if their physical parts can move. A cork pulled from a pipe releases water; a branch yanked down lowers a platform; a weak object snaps. The consequence follows the object’s mass, anchor, and state, not a magic result.

6. The frog sticks only to damp, rough, porous surfaces when contact is slow enough and the angle is climbable. While stuck, holding up climbs slowly; holding down slides deliberately; not holding slides down; moving sideways or being hit breaks grip. On slick or smooth surfaces the pads do not catch, and the frog slides off unless the surface has sticky bits. Too fast or wrong angle means it bounces or slides off.

7. The cat notices if the frog is in its line of sight and moving, or lands loudly nearby. Once noticing, it turns, steps closer on a slow, predictable cadence, and keeps watching until the frog breaks line of sight or stays still long enough for it to lose interest. If the cat gets close enough and the frog is exposed, it swats; this is not death. The swat bats the frog to the last safe perch.

8. Open ground is any stretch the heron can cover with its shadow: water, bare stone, roof, lawn. The heron patrols a fixed route, and its shadow marks where its attention is. It strikes when the frog is visible in open ground without cover and the heron has a clear line; its strike has a visible windup. Disturbing something else—splash, branch hit, wasp swarm—makes it look away, pausing the strike. If it catches the frog, it swallows briefly and coughs the frog onto a nearby safe perch.

9. Sprinklers are physical valves. Some have a visible valve the frog can yank; others are triggered by rain, pressure, or a nearby mechanism. None are truly without warning: a hiss, click, bulging hose, drip, or wetting patch appears just before. When active, they wet surfaces, make them slippery, knock small objects, push light objects, and start short currents; they stop when their valve closes or their short cycle ends.

10. Water is slow and weak for the frog. It can paddle and steer, but drag and gravity make it heavy; currents push along their flow and may carry the frog past edges. It cannot jump from water and must swim to a climbable edge; if the edge is slippery it may slide back. Fish patrol visible water, notice the frog in open water, and snap; being snapped causes a short swallow and the frog is coughed onto the nearest safe edge.

11. Rain barrels tip when the frog lands unevenly, yanks a loose lid, or water load shifts. Leaves sink when the frog stands on them too long, water fills them, or a yank damages them. Drainpipes gurgle and spit when water pressure changes, a cork is pulled, a sprinkler runs, or the frog blocks a vent. Each change opens or closes routes: tipping creates ramps and currents, sinking removes platforms, gurgling and spitting creates temporary currents or pushes objects.

12. Slippery surfaces reduce grip and cause sliding, not climbing. Sticky surfaces let pads catch and allow controlled climb and slide. Brittle surfaces break after one landing, yank, or weight shift. Moving surfaces carry the frog or change shape on their own rules. Wind applies lateral force to airborne frogs, visible by rain, vines, and hanging cloth; it changes hop arcs.

13. Eating is only what happens when the tongue catches an insect. The insect is swallowed immediately; no counter, upgrade, or required objective changes. The reaction is tied to the insect: fly makes a sneeze or back-hop, wasp makes a dizzy sideways stagger, and other insects follow the same pattern, small insects giving small reactions and irritating insects giving larger ones. A player may deliberately eat an insect to use that one-time reaction, but it cannot be saved, stacked, or farmed.

14. A safe perch is the last stable place the frog rested on for a moment while not in water, not on a moving hazard, not under an active cat, heron, or fish attack, and not on a crumbling or slippery edge. The game marks it with a faint wet ring. When a cat, heron, fish, or other danger removes the frog, it returns instantly to that perch with a small recovery hop; if that perch is no longer valid, it uses the nearest earlier valid perch.

15. The eyes and throat are the game’s danger language. The frog’s eyes turn toward threats and widen when a cat, heron, or fish has noticed, letting the player read attention without a meter. The throat swells and pulses as danger gets closer; it tells the player when to hide or move, but it does not add health, stamina, or special physics.
