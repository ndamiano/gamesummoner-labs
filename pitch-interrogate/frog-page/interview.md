# The interview

## Round 1

### Interrogator

THE GAME SO FAR
The game is a platformer about a small frog trying to climb through rooftops, gutters, rain barrels, garden edges, and wet walls to reach a moonlit pond where other frogs are already calling. The player does not run or fight; the frog moves by crouching, leaping, sticking to wet surfaces, hanging from twigs, and swinging with its tongue. The core pleasure is reading a wet, dangerous environment like a small animal would: choosing whether to catch a ledge, trust a twig, avoid a slick surface, or take a hidden route because the obvious one leads to being watched or swallowed. The player must commit to leap arcs and physical chains of movement, treating every gap as a route-finding problem about weight, grip, fragility, and attention.

SETTLED
You play as a small frog trying to get to the moonlit pond on the rooftops, where the other frogs are already calling.
You don’t run or fight your way there.
The frog crouches, leaps, sticks to wet walls, hangs from twigs, and swings through rain barrels, gutters, and garden edges with its tongue.
Every jump is an arc the player has to commit to.
Every gap asks the player to read the place like a frog: what can I catch, what will break, what is too slick, and what is watching me.
The game is about being small, slippery, and alive in a wet world.
The joy is finding the clever, physical route upward.
The obvious route would get you swallowed.

QUESTIONS
1. Is the moment-to-moment loop a short upward route where you plan and execute a chain of crouch, leap, stick, hang, and swing, and the challenge comes from committing to those physical actions without being able to casually correct mid-jump? My guess: yes, jumps are committed arcs, and cleverness comes from chaining small moves rather than fighting enemies.

2. Does the tongue work only as a locomotion and anchoring tool, such as sticking to wet surfaces, hanging from twigs, and grabbing rain barrels, gutters, or garden edges, rather than attacking, pulling objects, or collecting things? My guess: yes, “with your tongue” means the tongue is how the frog grips, hangs, and swings, and “what can I catch” means catching a ledge or support, not prey.

3. Are “what is watching me” and “being swallowed” implemented as predators or dangers that occupy obvious routes and punish careless paths, without the player fighting them? My guess: yes, watching creatures are avoidable hazards with visible attention or movement patterns, and the game is about slipping past them or choosing a harder but safer route.

4. Do different parts of the world behave differently according to grip, wetness, and breakage: wet walls can be stuck to, dry or smooth places are too slick, thin twigs or fragile edges can break under weight, and rain barrels or gutters can be swung from? My guess: yes, the environment is read through material state, and “too slick” or “what will break” is a core failure condition.

5. Does a run begin at a low, exposed part of a rooftop or garden and end when the frog reaches the next pond or calling frogs, with later routes becoming harder by adding more watching dangers, more fragile supports, more slick surfaces, and more complicated vertical chains? My guess: yes, the game is a sequence of traversal routes up a wet world, and difficulty rises by making the safe-looking route more lethal and the clever route more demanding.

### Pitcher

1. Yes. The moment-to-moment loop is short upward routes where the player plans and commits to a chain of crouch, leap, stick, hang, and swing, and the challenge is that once the frog leaves the ground it can’t casually correct; a bad jump means sliding, falling, or hanging for a beat before you rethink.

2. Mostly, but not only. The tongue is primarily for anchoring, hanging, swinging, and catching grabbable supports, not attacking enemies or filling a collectathon; it can also grab and pull small physical objects if that changes the scene physically, like disturbing a branch, pulling a cork, or catching a strand of spider silk.

3. Yes. Watching dangers occupy tempting routes and punish careless paths. Cats, herons, fish, sprinklers, and similar hazards are readable through attention or movement patterns, and the player avoids, distracts, waits, or takes a harder route. Getting caught is embarrassment and reset, not combat.

4. Yes. Grip, wetness, and breakage are the language of the level. Wet walls can be stuck to, smooth or dry surfaces are too slick, thin supports can break under weight or a bad tug, and sturdy edges, branches, or grabbable anchors can be swung from only if they are actually catchable.

5. Not exactly as a run that ends at the next pond. It is built as crafted levels or legs of the journey: each route moves the frog from a low place to a higher safe perch or new district, while the moonlit pond and the calling frogs remain the distant goal. Later levels raise difficulty by making the obvious path watched, fragile, slick, or broken, and the clever path demand longer, riskier physical chains.

QUESTIONS
1. Does crouch work as a posture that lowers the frog’s profile, helps it stay hidden or avoid being noticed by watching dangers, and changes the leap into a shorter, safer, or more controlled takeoff rather than a separate combat or movement ability? My guess: yes, crouching is for staying small, waiting out attention, and preparing a careful hop; it does not give a powerful dash or attack.

2. When a jump goes wrong, can the frog sometimes catch a late edge, slide, or hang for a beat before failing, but never steer freely in midair? My guess: yes, the frog may briefly cling or slide if it makes accidental contact, but it cannot fully correct a committed arc; failure is usually sliding off, hanging uselessly, or falling back down.

3. Does each level leg begin at a low, unsafe place and end at a higher safe perch or district boundary, with failure returning the player to the last safe perch or level start rather than a hidden checkpoint system? My guess: yes, safe perches are the real checkpoints, and getting caught or falling simply puts the frog back at the last place it was safe, with embarrassment but no combat.

4. Does “distract” mean using the tongue to pull or disturb small physical objects so that a watching danger turns its attention, reveals a route, or stops blocking a path? My guess: yes, pulling a cork, disturbing a branch, or catching silk creates a physical disturbance that makes the hazard look, move, or stop, but the frog still cannot fight it.

5. Are the first-day essentials the named verbs and named environment language: crouch, committed leap, stick, hang, swing, tongue-grabbing supports, wet/slippery/breakable materials, and at least one readable watching danger? My guess: yes, those are the core of the game from the first day; anything else is only a variation of those systems, not a new power or separate mode.

### Pitcher

1. Not as a stealth posture. Crouch is the coil that lets the player set up and aim a committed takeoff; avoiding attention comes from stillness, cover, and timing, not from entering a special hidden crouch state.

2. Yes. The frog can sometimes catch a late edge, slide, or dangle for a beat after a bad jump, but it never gets free midair steering; failure usually means losing the perch and having to rethink the route.

3. Yes. Each leg moves from a low, exposed place to a higher safe perch or district boundary, and failure returns the frog to the last safe perch, or to the leg start if it has not reached one yet.

4. Yes, but only through physical cause and effect. The tongue may disturb a branch, pull a cork, or trip a sprinkler so a hazard reacts, is delayed, or reveals a route; the frog still cannot fight or command the danger directly.

5. Yes. The first day is exactly those verbs and material cues: crouch, committed leap, stick, hang, swing, grabbable supports, wet/slippery/breakable surfaces, and at least one readable watching danger. Everything else is a variation on those systems, not a new power or separate mode.
