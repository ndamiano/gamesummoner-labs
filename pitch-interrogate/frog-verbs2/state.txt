Game prompt: a platformer where you play as a frog

the game: A small tree frog wakes on a lily in a rain barrel and must cross a rain-soaked rooftop garden to the Moon Pool before first light. The frog moves from rain barrels to gutters, windowsills, wires, awnings, and ledges, using shadow and cover to avoid watchers and fixed dangers, while the night changes the garden and drains the pool. One crossing is the whole game: if the frog is taken, the attempt resets to the start; if the frog enters the Moon Pool before it closes, the game records whether the rain lily bulbs were completed and returns to the start of the same crossing. There is no next crossing.

view: Side-on 2D platformer view, camera follows the frog and shows shadowed cover, open lit spaces, watcher attention, and the Moon Pool’s draining state.

time: real-time

a run: A run starts with the frog waking in the rain barrel. The attempt begins when the frog leaves the barrel and ends when the frog enters the Moon Pool, is taken by a hazard or watcher, or the pool closes. Failure returns to the start of the same crossing with the garden’s layout the same, the night reset, and any bulbs taken during that attempt lost. It becomes harder as the night moves: shadows shrink, lamps warm, watchers change where they look, drains and fans change state, hidden places become reachable or deadly, and the Moon Pool drains until its water is gone behind the pool grate and the greenhouse hatch shuts.

tone: It is about "being small in a place that notices you." The air is "wet and loud," the night "changes around you," and "every crossing is a decision about whether to be seen." The player learns which routes are quiet and which are worth the risk.

look and sound: Wet rooftop night at close range: rain-soaked surfaces, dripping water, drain steam, distant city hum, warm lamp glow, glass, shadowed ledges, and quiet stretches broken by watcher movement.

core mechanics:
- Platforming and route viability: The frog moves across connected surfaces. Rain barrels, gutters, windowsills, wires, awnings, ledges, and other platforms are usable when reachable. Open gutters are gaps. Loose tiles are usable only if they still hold. A route is possible when every surface needed is connected, reachable, and not covered by a watcher or fixed danger at that moment. A route is impossible if a gap is too wide, a surface is watched, a hazard blocks it, or a tile has been disturbed. The frog’s small size gives access to narrow gutters and thin ledges, but it cannot cross open lit spaces because watchers see it from above.
- Shadow, cover, and being seen: The player treats shadow and cover as the only safe ground. Waiting in shadow means staying in a place where no watcher’s sightline reaches. Hiding means breaking the sightline with cover or darkness. Being seen means a watcher notices the frog. A yard is quiet when no watcher is attending to that part of the garden. The player moves between safe places, waits while a watcher looks elsewhere, and hides until the watcher turns away.
- Watchers and attention: Watchers notice the frog when the frog is in a watcher’s sightline and not hidden. When that happens the watcher turns and covers the next surface with its attention, so the route the frog was using is no longer safe. Another way appears when the watcher turns away or moves enough to open a shadowed gap in its sightline. If the frog cannot reach shadow before the watcher’s attention reaches its surface, the watcher turns fully and takes the frog, ending the attempt.
- Fixed dangers: Fixed dangers are drains, lamps, broken glass, fans, and open gutters. “Takes” means the fixed danger removes the frog from the playable route and ends the attempt. A drain pulls or scalds, a lamp burns, broken glass cuts, a fan blows the frog off, and an open gutter drops. There is no damage state; the crossing is over. Drains and fans may change state; when active they take the frog, and when inactive their immediate route can be passable.
- Time cycle and night changes: The time cycle begins when the frog wakes and the crossing starts. As the night moves toward first light, shadows shrink, lamps warm, the heron and cat change where they look, drains and fans change state, and the Moon Pool drains. The player knows the pool has closed because the water is gone behind the pool grate and the greenhouse hatch shuts. If the night gets too far and the pool closes, the attempt fails at once.
- Attempt, failure, and reset: An attempt is one continuous crossing from the rain barrel start to the Moon Pool. It begins when the frog leaves the barrel and ends when the frog enters the pool, is lost, or the pool closes. If a hazard takes the frog, if a watcher takes the frog, or if the pool closes, the attempt ends and the frog returns to the start of that crossing. The garden’s layout is the same, the night resets, and any bulbs taken during that attempt are lost. There is no next crossing.
- Moon Pool goal and closure: The Moon Pool is the goal and is going to be drained when the first light comes. The game is complete when the frog enters the Moon Pool before it closes. Reaching it ends the game; after that the game shows only whether the rain lily bulbs were completed and returns to the start of the same crossing. There is no next crossing.
- Rain lily bulbs and set completion: Rain lily bulbs are hidden throughout the gardens as a set. Taking one marks it collected; they do not change the frog or open anything. If the player leaves them, the crossing can still be completed. If the player takes some, the game records only what was collected in that attempt. If the player completes the set and reaches the Moon Pool, the ending shows the completed set. If the attempt fails, those bulbs are lost.

supporting mechanics:
- Loose tile disturbance: A rain lily bulb glints under a loose tile. Taking that bulb collects it and disturbs the tile. The tile slips and falls into the gap below, so it is no longer a usable ledge. That short route is closed for the rest of the attempt, and the frog must use another way. When the attempt resets, the tile returns to its starting state with the night reset.
- Cat appearance event: The cat is a fixed event in the crossing, not a random spawn. It appears on the awning after the frog takes the first rain lily bulb and reaches the windowsill. If the frog does not take the bulb, the tile stays still and no cat appears in that attempt. If the crossing starts again later, the same trigger is live again: taking the bulb brings the cat to the awning. The cat sits between the opening teaching of the heron and the first safe wire crossing; once it is there, the awning is watched, and the frog must wait for it to turn away or take the lower route.
- Hidden places becoming reachable or deadly: A hidden place becomes reachable when a shadowed gap connects to it and no watcher, lamp, or fixed danger covers it. It becomes deadly when the night moves and brings attention or danger to it: a watcher turns toward it, a lamp lights it, a drain or fan switches on, or a loose tile falls.

left out: It is not a road-crossing game, not a combat game, not a story game with named characters, not a survival game, not a roguelike, not a game about vehicles, and not a game where the player builds or fights through the world. There is no next crossing, no health, no armor, no damage state, no permanent progression beyond recording whether the rain lily bulbs were completed, no random cat spawn, and no separate hazard for greenhouse glass except as represented by the Moon Pool’s closing hatch.
