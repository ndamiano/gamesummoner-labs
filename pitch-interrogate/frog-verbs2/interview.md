# The interview

## Round 1

### Interrogator

CRACKS

- The page says the frog must “cross from garden to garden and reach it,” but it does not say what reaching the Moon Pool means, what counts as completing the game, or what the player does afterward.

- “If the night changes too far, the Moon Pool is closed for the attempt” names an attempt, a closure, and a boundary without saying what they are, what starts them, or what ends them.

- “If a hazard takes the frog from the path, the crossing is lost and the path must be tried again” names a path, a crossing, and a retry without saying what those are or what changes when they fail.

- “If a watcher notices the frog, the watcher turns and the way is no longer there. The frog has to hide until another way appears” does not say what watching is, what makes a watcher notice, what a missing way is, or what another way is.

- The page names a “time cycle” and says “The same gardens change as the night moves,” but it does not say when the cycle starts, what changes during it, or how the player knows those changes are happening.

- The stealth language—“waits in shadow,” “moves when the yard is quiet,” “risk being seen,” “hide until another way appears”—has no mechanics explaining what those states are or how the player creates or avoids them.

- “Fixed dangers: drains, lamps, broken glass, fans, open gutters” are named, but the page does not say what they do, how the frog interacts with them, or what happens when the player meets them.

- The page names “platforming” and describes gutters, windowsills, wires, awnings, ledges, and loose tiles, but it does not say what movement possibilities exist or what makes a route possible or impossible.

- “Rain lily bulbs are hidden throughout the gardens as a set” and “the game keeps asking whether to collect them or go straight for the Moon Pool” give no rule for what bulbs do, what a set means, or what happens if the player ignores or completes them.

- “Then a cat appears on the awning” has no trigger, timing, or place in the order of the game.

- “The fact that the frog is small and exposed” is named as something working against the frog, but it is not expressed as a playable rule.

QUESTIONS

1. When the frog reaches the Moon Pool, what does the game consider complete, and what happens next?

2. What is an “attempt” in the sentence “If the night changes too far, the Moon Pool is closed for the attempt,” and what happens when the Moon Pool is closed?

3. What is “the path” or “the crossing” in “If a hazard takes the frog from the path, the crossing is lost and the path must be tried again,” and what changes or remains when it is lost?

4. How does a watcher notice the frog, and what do “the way is no longer there” and “another way appears” change in the garden?

5. What starts the time cycle, what changes as “the night moves,” and what tells the player that the Moon Pool has closed?

6. What does it mean for the frog to wait in shadow, hide, be seen, or for the yard to be quiet, and what can the player do about those states?

7. What do fixed dangers such as drains, lamps, broken glass, fans, and open gutters do to the frog, and what can the player do about them?

8. What makes a platforming route possible or impossible for the frog across rain barrels, gutters, windowsills, wires, awnings, ledges, and loose tiles?

9. What do rain lily bulbs do when taken, and what happens if the player leaves them, takes some, or completes the set?

10. What causes “Then a cat appears on the awning” to happen, and where does that event sit in the sequence of a crossing?

11. How does “the fact that the frog is small and exposed” become something the player has to manage in the game?

### Pitcher

1. The game is complete when the frog enters the Moon Pool before it closes. Reaching it ends the game; after that the game shows only whether the rain lily bulbs were completed and returns to the start of the same crossing. There is no next crossing.

2. An attempt is one continuous crossing from the rain barrel start to the Moon Pool. It begins when the frog leaves the barrel and ends when the frog enters the pool, is lost, or the pool closes. If the night gets too far and the pool closes, the attempt fails at once and the game returns to the start of that crossing.

3. “The path” is the route the frog is taking from the start of the crossing to the Moon Pool. “The crossing” is that attempt. If a hazard takes the frog, the attempt ends and the frog returns to the start of the crossing; the garden is the same, but the night resets and any bulbs taken during that attempt are lost.

4. A watcher notices the frog when the frog is in a watcher’s sightline and not hidden in shadow or cover. When that happens the watcher turns and covers the next surface with its attention, so the route the frog was using is no longer safe. “Another way appears” means the watcher turns away or moves enough to open a shadowed gap in its sightline.

5. The time cycle begins with the frog waking in the rain barrel and the crossing starting. As the night moves toward first light, shadows shrink, lamps warm, the heron and cat change where they look, drains and fans change state, and the Moon Pool drains. The player knows the pool has closed because the water is gone behind the pool grate and the greenhouse hatch shuts.

6. To wait in shadow is to stay in a place where no watcher’s sightline reaches; to hide is to break the sightline with cover or darkness; to be seen is to be noticed by a watcher; the yard is quiet when no watcher is attending to that part of the garden. The player can move between safe places, wait while a watcher looks elsewhere, and hide until the watcher turns away.

7. Fixed dangers hurt on touch and usually end the crossing: drains pull or scald, lamps burn, broken glass cuts, fans blow the frog off, and open gutters drop. The player must avoid them or get past them only where there is a gap, a shadowed route, or a moment when the danger is not touching the way.

8. A route is possible when every surface the frog needs to use is connected, reachable, and not covered by a watcher or fixed danger at that moment. Gutters, windowsills, awnings, ledges, wires, and rain barrels are platforms; open gutters are gaps; loose tiles are usable only if they still hold. A route is impossible if a gap is too wide, a surface is watched, a hazard blocks it, or a tile has been disturbed.

9. Rain lily bulbs are only the set. Taking one marks it collected; they do not change the frog or open anything. If the player leaves them, the crossing can still be completed. If the player takes some, the game records only what was collected in that attempt. If the player completes the set and reaches the Moon Pool, the ending shows the completed set; if the attempt fails, those bulbs are lost.

10. The cat is a fixed event in the first crossing, not a random spawn. It appears on the awning after the frog takes the first rain lily bulb and reaches the windowsill. It sits between the opening teaching of the heron and the first safe wire crossing: once it is there, the awning is watched, and the frog must wait for it to turn away or take the lower route.

11. “Small and exposed” means the frog has no health, no armor, and no way to survive being seen; it can only move from shadow to shadow. Its size gives access to narrow gutters and thin ledges, but it cannot cross open lit spaces because watchers see it from above. The player must treat shadow and cover as the only safe ground.

## Round 2

### Interrogator

CRACKS
- The page makes being seen a problem but does not say what happens when the frog is noticed and cannot hide: “If a watcher notices the frog, the watcher turns and the way is no longer there. The frog has to hide until another way appears.”
- The page makes hazards end crossings but does not say what “takes” means for each fixed danger: “If a hazard takes the frog from the path.”
- The page names a loose tile and a bulb under it but does not say how taking the bulb changes the tile or whether the tile remains usable: “A rain lily bulb glints under a loose tile. The frog takes it.”
- The page presents a cat appearing as a fixed first-moment event but does not say what starts it, whether it depends on taking the bulb, or whether it happens on later attempts: “Then a cat appears on the awning.”
- The page lists lamps as fixed dangers and says lights change during the night, but does not say what a changed lamp does to routes or the frog: “Fixed dangers: drains, lamps” / “lights change.”
- The page says hidden places change with the night but does not say what makes them reachable or deadly: “hidden places become reachable or deadly.”

QUESTIONS
1. In “If a watcher notices the frog, the watcher turns and the way is no longer there. The frog has to hide until another way appears,” what happens to the frog and the attempt if the frog is noticed and does not reach a place where it can hide?
2. What does “If a hazard takes the frog from the path” mean during play, and what different things can happen when the frog meets a fixed danger?
3. What does taking “A rain lily bulb glints under a loose tile. The frog takes it” do to the tile, the route, and the attempt?
4. What rule starts “Then a cat appears on the awning,” and what happens if the frog does not take the bulb in that opening or begins the crossing again later?
5. When the page says “Fixed dangers: drains, lamps” and later “lights change,” what does a lamp changing state do to the playable route?
6. When the page says “hidden places become reachable or deadly,” what makes a hidden place become reachable or deadly?

### Pitcher

1. If a watcher notices the frog and the frog cannot reach shadow before the watcher’s attention reaches its surface, the watcher turns fully and takes the frog. The attempt ends, and the game returns to the start of that crossing.

2. “Takes” means the fixed danger removes the frog from the playable route and ends the attempt. Each danger does this in its own way: a drain pulls or scalds, a lamp burns, broken glass cuts, a fan blows the frog off, an open gutter drops. There is no damage state; the crossing is over.

3. Taking that bulb collects it and disturbs the tile. The tile slips and falls into the gap below, so it is no longer a usable ledge. That short route is closed for the rest of the attempt, and the frog must use another way.

4. The cat appears when the frog takes that first bulb and reaches the windowsill. If the frog does not take the bulb, the tile stays still and no cat appears in that attempt. If the crossing starts again later, the same trigger is live again: taking the bulb brings the cat to the awning.

5. A lamp changes between cold/dark and warm/lit. When cold, its nearby shadow is safe to use, though the fixture is still a fixed danger if touched. When it warms or lights, the lamp itself and any surface under its light become deadly or watched, closing shadowed routes until it cools.

6. A hidden place becomes reachable when a shadowed gap connects to it and no watcher, lamp, or fixed danger covers it. It becomes deadly when the night moves and brings attention or danger to it: a watcher turns toward it, a lamp lights it, a drain or fan switches on, or a loose tile falls.
