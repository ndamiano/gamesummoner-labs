Game prompt: a platformer where you play as a frog

the game: A side-view platformer where a small pale-bellied frog crosses a rain-swollen marsh toward the old nursery pool. The player walks, short-hops, long-hops, lands, turns, and ducks across lily pads, half-sunk logs, reed ledges, wet stepping stones, and low mushroom caps while fixed hazards bite: cattail thorns, slick oil patches, snapping turtle jaws, and sharp reed bundles. Herons stand at the far end of the pond and scan with moving sight lines; the frog must move in bursts, then stop, then move again, and fully hide or leave a sight line before a warning becomes a dive. Deep water is not instant death but the current carries the frog toward safe edges, and shallow water is only a failed landing. The time cycle runs once in real time from dusk through deep dusk into night, then keeps changing through night: water rises, pads sink or rise, oil becomes harder to see, and heron sight lines remain active but harder to read. Moonflies are optional collectibles that permanently reveal hidden stepping stones and secret crossings, opening a harder alternate route that the time cycle may alter but never make mandatory.

view: A side-view platformer camera follows the frog across a pond segment, keeping the relevant ledges, hazards, heron perches, pale glint sight lines, safe-ledge moss, and water depth readable enough for timed movement and hiding.

time: Real-time.

a run: A run starts when the frog enters a marsh pond segment at dusk from an entry ledge kept dry, hazard-free, and outside active heron sight lines. The goal is the old nursery pool on the far side. The run ends when the frog reaches the old nursery pool or leaves that pond segment; leaving ends the run and its time cycle. It becomes harder as the real-time clock passes fixed stage boundaries into dusk, deep dusk, and night, then continues changing through night by raising water, sinking or raising pads, reducing oil visibility, dimming heron sight-line cues, and altering optional moonfly routes.

tone: “You’re a small frog trying to cross a dusk marsh to the old nursery pool. You hop from lily pad to reed ledge, staying quiet under the herons’ gaze while fixed hazards bite and hidden moonflies mark secret routes. As night falls, the marsh rearranges itself, so the safe path you learned becomes a gamble.”

look and sound: The look is a rain-swollen marsh moving from dusk into night, with a pale-bellied frog, faint pale moss marking safe ledges, pale glints across water and reeds for heron sight lines, moonfly glows, and reduced visual contrast at night; sounds are wet landings, heron caws, splashes, current ripples, and clear hazard-timing cues.

core mechanics:
- Frog Movement: The player controls a small pale-bellied frog with ordinary platforming controls under gravity: walk, short-hop, long-hop, land, turn, and duck. Continuous movement is allowed, but the game is built around “committed hops”: each gap is placed so it requires one deliberate jump with no extra movement option to rescue a bad takeoff. The game encourages “move in bursts, then stop, then move again” because moving inside a heron sight line fills notice, and some pads tilt under continuous movement. Ducking means fully hiding behind an occluder or under a low pad so no part of the frog is visible.
- Platforms and Safe Ledges: Lily pads, half-sunk logs, reed ledges, wet stepping stones, and low mushroom caps are the crossing surfaces. A ledge or edge is safe only when it is solid, dry, outside any active heron sight line, not touching a hazard, and not currently tilting or sinking. Safe ledges are marked with faint pale moss. The “last safe ledge” is the most recently occupied safe ledge. When a heron dive or hazard hit would reset the frog, the game first checks the last safe ledge; if it is no longer safe, it searches backward along the path the frog came from and uses the nearest currently safe ledge. If no such ledge exists, it returns the frog to the entry ledge of that pond segment. The entry ledge is kept dry, hazard-free, and outside active heron sight lines. The frog is never dropped into water, onto a tilting pad, inside a hazard, or directly under a live heron sight line.
- Heron Stealth: Herons do not walk around the pond. Each heron is fixed to its perch at the far side; what moves is its head and the sight line it casts, which sweeps through a learnable arc. Each heron has its own visibility meter. At dusk, the sight line is shown as a pale glint across water and reeds and is readable. The meter fills only when the frog moves inside that sight line. If the frog stops inside a sight line, the meter stops filling but does not drain; the danger remains at whatever level it reached. If the frog fully hides or leaves the sight line, the meter drains. If it drains before the warning, nothing happens. If it fills, the heron caws and gives warning. During a warning, the frog may still move, but it must be fully hidden before the caw ends. If hidden before the caw ends, the heron aborts, the meter resets, and the frog is not punished. If not hidden, the heron dives and the frog is knocked back to the last safe ledge, or the safe-search fallback if that ledge is no longer safe. If the frog re-enters a sight line while the meter is still active, it begins filling again; if it re-enters or fails to hide during the warning, the dive happens. If one heron’s meter fills, that one begins the warning. If another heron would also fill while that warning is live, it is suppressed; the first warning is the one that matters. If the frog is not hidden, that one heron dives, and all active warnings in that pond segment are cleared and reset. If two fill at the same instant, the one whose sight line is closest to the frog triggers, and the others reset. If the frog hides or leaves all sight lines before any warning resolves, every active notice meter drains to zero. Later stages dim the glint and make the sight line harder to read, but the sight line is still there and still fills notice when the frog moves inside it.
- hazards: A system of dangers fixed in place in a level, which hurt the player on touch and must be avoided or got past. In this game, hazards are fixed in place and hurt on touch. The player is never meant to fight them; the player must read them, wait out their timing, and pass them. A hazard hit does not kill and does not cost a life. It breaks the player’s current chain of hops through that hazard set and returns the frog to the last safe ledge before the hazard. “Costing the line” means losing the uninterrupted run through that hazard sequence. “Bounced out of the route” means the frog is flung or pushed back to that safe ledge, losing forward progress. Cattail thorns are fixed hazards that hurt on touch and cause a hazard hit. Snapping turtle jaws are timed hazards: when open, touching them causes a hazard hit; when closed, they can be jumped over or stood on until they open. Sharp reed bundles are landing hazards: if the frog’s feet touch one, the landing is rejected and the frog is pushed back to the last safe ledge. Slick oil patches are traction hazards: touching one removes control and slides the frog along the slick; if the slide reaches water, the current takes over; if the slide would otherwise stop on a ledge, the player has still lost the line and is returned to the last safe ledge.
- Water and Current: Water is split by functional depth. A pool is shallow if its bottom is within the frog’s maximum jump height from the water surface; it is deep if the bottom is below that. Falling into shallow water is a failed landing but not a current event: the frog lands on the bottom, can turn, and can jump out to a nearby safe edge or platform. Falling into deep water starts a carried state. Deep water has no foothold. The frog can turn and paddle weakly but cannot jump until it reaches an edge. The current follows visible ripples and carries the frog toward the nearest safe edge. The player can steer slightly toward that edge. While being carried, the frog is in a transport state, not a normal play state: it cannot jump, can steer only weakly toward the intended safe edge, and cannot be affected by hazards or herons. Hazards are passed through without triggering. Heron sight lines do not fill notice or trigger dives. If the current would carry the frog to an edge that has become unsafe, that edge is not considered; the current abandons it and selects the next nearest safe edge. If the current carries past the intended safe edge, or if no safe edge is available, the frog is deposited at the entry ledge of that pond segment.
- time cycle: A system where the time of day or the season moves on in the game world and changes what can happen there. In this game, the time cycle advances in real time while the frog is in play. The level begins at dusk when the player enters the marsh. It moves through fixed stages: dusk, deep dusk, and night. Each stage begins when the clock reaches its threshold and ends when the next threshold is reached. The moment “the marsh changes what it asks” is exactly one of these stage boundaries, marked by a shift in light. The cycle does not stop at night and does not loop back to dusk. It runs once through the crossing: dusk, deep dusk, night. After the night boundary, night itself keeps changing continuously: water rises further, pads continue to sink or rise, and oil stays hard to see, so the marsh remains in the middle of change. The overall cycle begins when the frog enters the marsh, and it ends when the crossing ends, either by reaching the old nursery pool or by leaving that pond segment. At each stage boundary, the world states change directly. Water rises by raising the water line around low ledges. Some pads sink by lowering into the water; others rise by lifting into a new reachable position. If the frog is on a pad that sinks, it falls into the water, and the depth rule applies. If a pad rises, it may create a new crossing or expose the frog to a heron sight line. Oil keeps the same collision and behavior, but its visual contrast is reduced. Rising water does not drown the frog. At each stage boundary, any surface whose top is below the water line becomes submerged and is removed from the safe-surface list. If the frog is standing on such a surface when that happens, it loses footing and falls into the water at that spot. Then the depth rule applies: shallow water lets the frog land and jump out; deep water starts the current. Sinking pads and over-tilted pads are resolved the same way: the surface stops being a footing, and the frog is dropped into water. A surface becoming unsafe only because a heron sight line moves onto it does not move the frog; it only makes notice possible.

supporting mechanics:
- Moonflies and Hidden Routes: Hidden around the marsh are moonflies, a collectibles set tucked into hollow reeds, under lifted pads, behind fallen logs, and in places that require a risky detour. They are not needed to finish, but they form a set the player can choose to hunt. Collecting a moonfly permanently reveals one hidden route: its associated stepping stones or secret crossing become visible as a faint glow. The time cycle does not reveal new routes on its own; it only makes already revealed routes easier to see and follow. Moonflies give the information, and the time cycle increases its visibility. The moonfly route is optional. It is an alternate, harder crossing through the same marsh, not required to finish. The normal route always remains available. The time cycle can block it, make it harder, or temporarily remove it by sinking stones, raising water, or changing sight lines, but it never becomes mandatory.
- Tilting Lily Pads: A tilting lily pad leans toward the frog’s weight and movement. Landing off-center starts a tilt; standing still off-center increases it; moving across it changes the tilt in the direction of movement. If the tilt becomes too steep, the frog slides into the water and the current takes it. The player can counterbalance by moving opposite the tilt or jump before it becomes too steep. This changes the platforming challenge from simply landing on a pad to balancing, timing, and choosing where to land. A tilting pad can be stood on, but while it is tilting it is not a safe ledge.
