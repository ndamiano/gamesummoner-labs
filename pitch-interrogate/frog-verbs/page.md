# The pitcher's page

Ask: `a platformer where you play as a frog`

## My page

The game is made of platforming, hazards, collectibles, stealth, and time cycle.

A small pale-bellied frog starts at the edge of a rain-swollen marsh. The first moments are simple and physical: the player walks, short-hops, long-hops, lands, turns, and ducks across lily pads, half-sunk logs, reed ledges, wet stepping stones, and low mushroom caps. The controls are ordinary platforming controls under gravity. The frog is after the old nursery pool on the far side, and the path across is a chain of small committed hops.

What works against the player is the marsh watching them. Herons stand at the far end of the pond and scan for movement. If they notice the frog, they caw and give warning; if the player does not get under a pad or flatten behind a reed, the heron dives and the frog is knocked back to the last safe ledge. The player must move in bursts, then stop, then move again. That is the stealth part: not hiding in darkness, but staying still and staying out of sight while something is actively looking.

The hazards are fixed in place and hurt on touch: cattail thorns, slick oil patches, snapping turtle jaws that open and shut in the shallows, and sharp reed bundles that snag a bad landing. The player is never meant to fight them. The player is meant to read them, wait out their timing, and pass them. Falling into deep water is not instant death, but it costs momentum: the current washes the frog back toward the nearest safe edge. A heron strike costs progress. A hazard hit costs the line.

Hidden around the marsh are moonflies, a collectibles set tucked into hollow reeds, under lifted pads, behind fallen logs, and in places that require a risky detour. They are not needed to finish, but they form a set the player can choose to hunt. When collected, their glow reveals hidden stepping stones and secret crossings, opening a harder, more graceful route through the same marsh.

The time cycle runs from dusk into night. As the light changes, the marsh changes what it asks. At first, the herons are visible and their sight lines are readable. Later, their shadows become harder to see, the water rises slightly, some pads sink, others rise, oil becomes harder to spot, and the moonflies’ hidden paths become clearer. The player cannot simply memorize a safe path and repeat it blindly; the same pond becomes a different crossing as night settles.

When it goes wrong, the game is clear about the cost. A missed hop drops the frog into water and pushes them back. A hazard hit bounces them out of the route. A heron that notices and finds the frog exposed resets the player to the last safe ledge. The failure is always tied to timing, exposure, or a bad landing, not to a hidden stat or a confusing rule.

After the player has been playing for a while, the game stays interesting because the marsh is always in the middle of changing. The player learns heron patterns, hazard rhythms, and which ledges are safe at different light levels. Then the time cycle changes the meaning of that knowledge. A route that was safe at dusk becomes exposed at night. A hidden moonfly path may open a shortcut that avoids the herons but runs through cattail thorns. The player is not just crossing the pond; they are choosing when to commit.

The particular things in the game are the pale-bellied frog, the tilting lily pads, the half-sunk logs, the reed ledges, the cattail thorns, the oil slicks, the snapping turtle jaws, the heron shadows, the moonfly glows, and the old nursery pool at the far end.

This game is not a combat game. It is not a story game with named characters. It is not a survival game about feeding or thirst. It is not a creature-collection game, a vehicle game, or a puzzle game where the player pushes blocks around. The player is a frog, and the game is a platformer about moving through a watching, changing wetland.

## Pitch

You’re a small frog trying to cross a dusk marsh to the old nursery pool. You hop from lily pad to reed ledge, staying quiet under the herons’ gaze while fixed hazards bite and hidden moonflies mark secret routes. As night falls, the marsh rearranges itself, so the safe path you learned becomes a gamble.
